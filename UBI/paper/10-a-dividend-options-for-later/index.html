A dividend is a yearly payment to every adult, the most visible universal element a basic income could have. The 22 October paper set out four ways to pay one and left the choice to Parliament. This revision recommends none of them now, for three reasons drawn from the paper's own figures. No dividend reduces poverty: Stage 1 gives the same poverty rate with or without one, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the poverty line with it (Table 10.4). After the taxes that pay for it, about a third of households are better off under every option, the same share as with no dividend (Table 10.2). And every option scores below Stage 1 alone on the paper's net-benefit method (Table 10.2).

This chapter sets the options out neutrally, with the tests any of them should pass before Parliament legislates it. Every table in it was run on the 22 October design, Part 1, with the option shown; none has been rerun on P*, and on P* the figures, especially the levy dates, would differ.

What a fund could and could not do

If Parliament later wants a fund, its case is narrower than the 22 October paper made it. A fund is public wealth: the wealth-tax fund would hold A$325.3bn at 30 June 2046 (A$203.5bn in 2026 dollars), and in a declared displacement it could lend the Budget up to a quarter of its balance, A$56.6bn in 2035 or A$75.6bn in 2040 (Table 10.2). That loan bridges about a year (Table 6.51). A fund is not much of a source of income. The wealth-tax fund's dividend is A$47 in 2029-30 and A$461 in 2045-46, and on the high reading of the wealth tax's losses in other taxes the fund runs out in 2083-84 (Table 10.24). Its return is a risk the recipients bear: at the bond rate it pays A$383 in 2045-46 and is exhausted by 2067-68 (Table 10.12). A surplus fund lasts at the central return but pays a token dividend, A$1 in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46.

Universal cash tied to volatile revenue has been cut or eroded where it has been tried. Alaska's Governor halved the 2016 dividend by veto, and the state Supreme Court upheld the cut [S99, S195]; Alaska's 2025 dividend was its smallest in real terms [S229]. Iran's near-universal transfer lost purchasing power to inflation and sanctions [S100].

The tests any dividend should pass

Before Parliament legislates any dividend, the paper proposes five tests.

  1. A PBO costing of the option against the 2026-27 Budget baseline, and for a fund a ruling on its Budget treatment (Recommendation 12c).
  2. A stated value on universality. Parliament states what it values paying every adult at, per adult a year, and the option's break-even weight must be below it. On the 22 October run the weights are A$171 to A$657 for the wealth-tax fund, A$22 to A$65 for the Budget-paid dividend and A$20 to A$37 for the surplus fund (Table 10.2).
  3. Financing separate from Stage 1's. The option's financing is stated separately from the five durable taxes, with the levy it adds and the year it brings Stage 1's first levy forward to. On the 22 October run the dividend options brought it from 2044-45 to between 2038-39 and 2040-41 (Table 10.2).
  4. Rules described as statutory. Any never-cut guarantee and any rule protecting a fund's capital are statutory, and a later Parliament can change them; the Act would say so.
  5. Delivery reported. Lodgers are paid at assessment and non-lodgers by Services Australia after enrolment, with take-up reported (80% is assumed, with no Australian precedent; Table 10.15); the dividend is excluded from every adjusted-taxable-income test (Table 10.16).

The four options

A dividend would be paid every year to every eligible adult. It would not reduce poverty: on the 22 October run Stage 1 gives 11.3% on the relative line with or without a dividend, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the line (Table 10.4). It buys universality instead: a payment to every adult, a stake in any fund and a rail to people who lodge no tax return. Before the taxes are counted it reaches 86% of households rather than 60%; after them, about a third are better off under every option (32.8% to 33.0%; Table 10.2).

There are four options, and the paper recommends none of them now (Recommendation 4):

  • a wealth-tax fund: a Permanent AI Dividend Fund fed by the net wealth tax, the design the 22 October paper modelled in full (Tables 10.8 to 10.27);
  • a Budget-paid dividend: the same dividend paid from the five durable taxes and the levy, with no wealth tax and no fund;
  • a surplus fund: a permanent fund with no wealth tax, fed only by the durable account's surpluses;
  • no dividend: Stage 1 alone, which this paper recommends (P*; on the 22 October run, Part 1 alone).

Tables 10.2 and 10.3 compare them. With no dividend or the Budget-paid dividend the five taxes' surpluses reduce debt and are drawn before any levy, so the A$49.5bn kept meets Part 1 alone's shortfalls from 2038-39 until 2044-45; under the fund options each year's shortfall is levied (Tables 10.2 and 6.6).

Any dividend would follow the newly arrived resident's four-year waiting period that applies to JobSeeker Payment [S123]. Under any option lodgers would receive the dividend when their return is assessed, and Services Australia would pay the 80% of non-lodgers assumed to enrol, an assumption with no Australian precedent: 1.22 million of 1.53 million (Tables 10.15 and 6.25). Displaced workers lodge returns, so the rail does not reach them. The dividend is taxable, with the tax-free threshold and the Low Income Tax Offset (LITO) unchanged [S130, S131], and excluded from every test that uses adjusted taxable income, at A$10m to A$116m a year (Table 10.16). Every adult keeps at least 53.0 cents of each dollar (Table 10.1), so before funding no adult is worse off.

The wealth-tax fund receives the wealth tax net of the PBO's interaction line, and any surplus of the other five taxes after Stage 1's costs (Table 10.8). It is invested as the Future Fund is, at a central return of the consumer price index (CPI) plus 4%, the bottom of that fund's mandate [S132]. Each year it pays out 4% of its average balance over three years, less 10% kept in a reserve. The Act would forbid spending the capital on the dividend and hold the dividend in cash terms until a year's levied top-up would exceed 25% of it.

The dividend would be A$47 in 2029-30, about 90 cents a week, and A$461 in 2045-46 (Table 10.10). The wealth tax raises A$193.9bn gross to 2038-39, but the PBO's interaction line, the revenue it costs in other taxes, outlasts it [S133]. With all of that line charged, a high reading because part of it comes from a measure outside the stack, the tax's net present value is about A$8.5bn at the fund's 6.5% and negative at the debt rate (Table 10.22). The guarantee lapses in 2041-42, after A$1.5bn of levied top-ups (Table 10.23). The dividend is then held at A$461 to 2070-71 and falls to A$2 by 2085-86, once the line, charged to the fund's capital, has exhausted the fund in 2083-84 and the levy carries it (Table 10.24). With three-quarters or half of the line charged to the fund, the guarantee would last to 2049-50 or 2067-68 and the fund to 2095-96 or 2113-14, and the options' ranking is unchanged (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-107).

In 2028-29 the wealth-tax fund receives A$26.6bn, almost all of it wealth tax, with a deadweight cost of A$6.6bn to A$26.4bn (Table 10.6). Counting the taxes' economic cost and Stage 1's own behavioural costs, Part 1 alone has a net benefit of −A$24.9bn to −A$4.1bn, against −A$51.4bn to −A$10.7bn with the wealth-tax fund (Table 10.5).

Most of the difference comes from the wealth tax rather than from paying every adult. The Budget-paid dividend pays the same dividend from the durable taxes, whose shortfalls are levied from 2040-41, A$99.8bn to 2045-46 (Table 10.5). Over ten years it adds A$21.3bn of net cost but no deadweight cost, and on the complete accounting it breaks even at A$22 to A$65 per adult a year (Table 10.2). The wealth-tax fund costs A$239 to A$926 per adult a year over ten years, all of it the wealth tax's price (Table 10.7). The wealth tax buys a common asset and a lender in a displacement; without a fund, Stage 2 and 3 rises would be about 0.2 and 0.4 points higher (author's calculation from Table 6.39).

The surplus fund keeps a fund and its rules but drops the wealth tax. Fed only by the durable taxes' surpluses, it stays small, A$63.7bn in 2046, and pays A$1 in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46. It lasts at the central return, with its never-cut rule intact and a dividend of A$188 by 2085-86, but that is its best case: the rule lapses in 2089-90 at the bond rate and by 2065-66 in a fifth of 1,000 volatile return paths (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-108). Because surpluses build the fund rather than reduce debt, the levy starts in 2038-39 and totals A$77.3bn to 2045-46; the option breaks even at A$20 to A$37 per adult a year (Table 10.2).

The choice turns on the value Parliament places on universality, its tolerance for a levy, and whether it wants a permanent fund. A real dividend at a low price points to the Budget-paid dividend, and a lasting common asset to the surplus fund, with a token dividend. The largest dividend from a fund points to the wealth-tax fund, at the highest price, and no value on universality to Part 1 alone. The paper sets out the evidence and recommends none now (Recommendation 4).

Who gains and who loses after funding with a dividend

With the wealth-tax fund and its six taxes, only 33% of households are better off in 2028-29 and 67% are worse off on the central allocation (Table 10.26), or about 30% to 70% across the incidence variants. The losers are mostly in the top three deciles, where the taxes fall, but include 2.79 million bottom-half households and 85% of households with a member aged 67 or over. Across the variants that range is 1.15 million to 3.03 million, the lower figures if the capital-type taxes fall only on people with capital income (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-104). Over 2028-29 to 2045-46, in present value, 34% of households are better off (Table 10.27); at Stage 2, 50% are worse off, and at Stage 3, 60%.

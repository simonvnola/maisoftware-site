Australia should legislate a three-stage Australian Basic Income (ABI) so income support is ready if artificial intelligence (AI) displaces many workers. Part 1, a floor for working-age payments at the poverty line (A$569.81 a week) and a child payment, starts on 1 July 2028. It costs A$62.0bn in 2028-29, A$126.8bn over the forward estimates and A$664.1bn to 2036-37. Five taxes on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel pay for it, with a Budget advance. From 2038-39 they fall short each year; surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a small levy in 2044-45, which any yield below 99.7% of costings brings forward. Part 1 cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to between 11.3% and 11.8% and no current recipient loses income. Larger stages need evidence of AI job losses, a Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) certificate and a vote; Stage 3 cannot be funded from income tax.

Part 2, a yearly dividend for every adult, is left to Parliament. No dividend reduces poverty, and after the taxes about a third of households gain under each. A wealth-tax fund, built from a tax on fortunes above A$100 million, pays A$461 a year by 2045-46 but costs most and may run out. A Budget-paid dividend pays the same at a far lower price but needs the largest levy. A surplus fund pays A$1 in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46. Part 1 alone has the best net benefit and smallest levy but no universal element.

The problem

AI's early effects on jobs are uncertain [S2], and JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412 a week against a A$570 poverty line [S3, S4].

The proposal

Each child attracts a A$7,452 supplement. Families below A$72,631 get it on top of their maximum Family Tax Benefit, withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar with no cliffs; above a A$144,584 threshold near median parental income, families keep the supplement alone, withdrawn at 18 cents (the middle-income supplement), both assessed on past income. This leaves 49,500 families a year with a debt, and 49% of partnered earners get no floor if displaced.

  • Counting the taxes' economic cost and its behavioural costs, Part 1's net benefit is −A$24.9bn to −A$4.1bn in 2028-29 (−A$32.5bn to −A$5.8bn charging the start-up advance) and −A$48.3bn to −A$6.5bn a year as a level amount of equal present value to 2045-46.
  • In the model, child poverty falls from 15.6% to 7.2%, but poverty rises at 67 and over (11.1% to 12.1%–13.5%), among couples without children and in Disability Support Pension (63.6% to 68.2%) and Age Pension households.
  • After the taxes, 67% of households are worse off (30% to 70% across incidence variants).
  • At Part 1's budget a targeted package has 0.56 million to 0.61 million fewer people in poverty and a net benefit −A$0.3bn to A$4.1bn a year higher. It puts more higher earners at high tax rates; second earners get a lower mean rate but more often 50% or more. Swapping that supplement for a 75% Rent Assistance rise, Part 1 would do better on nearly every measure: 0.32 million fewer people in poverty and A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year more net benefit. The paper keeps it, at that price, because a universal Stage 2 pays every child; Parliament could choose otherwise.
  • The work-effort cap (a trial participation fall over 3 points) stops Stage 2 with probability 0.86 to 0.91.

Key risks

  1. Revenue shortfall: at 80% of costings Part 1 alone needs a levy from 2032-33, peaking at 4.41%.
  2. Stronger taxpayer response: at twice the PBO's assumption no Stage 3 rate works.
  3. Early AI displacement: with no fund to lend, Part 1 alone's levy in a severe shock is 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46, against nil and 5.87% with the wealth-tax fund.

Decisions needed now (Table 9.1)

  1. Pass the ABI Act with Part 1 by 31 December 2027; choose Part 2 after PBO costings.
  2. Refer this paper to a joint select committee reporting by 30 September 2027.
  3. Legislate the five durable taxes and yearly PBO certificates; vote separately on later stages.

3. A picture of 2045

Chapter 3 of 10 of the paper Beyond Work. It describes what Australia could look like in 2045 under the proposal, told through seven people and the aggregate numbers. It is for readers who want to see the intended result.

By 2045 a basic income could let Australians take up care, learning, creative work and community life without first proving need or looking for work, but only in a scenario the paper cannot promise. AI must displace enough work to meet the need test, Parliament must vote for Stage 2 and keep it, and the Australian trial must find a fall in participation under 3.0 points. A full pension-rate basic income also needs AI-driven growth far above the Budget's baseline and a later Act setting a Stage 3 rate (Section 6). On the central path Australia in 2045 still has Stage 1: the floor, the child payment and, if Parliament chooses the wealth-tax fund, a dividend of A$461 (Table 6.7b).

The seven people below are fictional and live on one path. To illustrate one of the Part 2 options, Parliament has chosen the wealth-tax fund. A severe AI shock from 2032-33 meets the need test early in 2035, and the counter-cyclical rule holds the floor and the dividend until then. Stage 2 starts in July 2035 with a rise of 25.5 points, and there is no Stage 3 by 2045-46 (path F, Table 6.7f). Duc and Tom are single, so the floor reaches them; a displaced worker whose partner earns above its cut-out would get only the dividend.

Seven Australians in 2045

Four of the seven gain clearly from Stage 2, though one of them found the transition hard; two gain little, and one pays much more tax.

Leanne, 52, cares for her mother, who has dementia, for most of each day in Toowoomba. Her basic income arrives every fortnight whatever her hours, but Carer Payment is above the Stage 2 rate, so most of her income still comes as a Carer Payment top-up under the same rules. In 2022, 3.0 million Australians (11.9%) were carers and 1.2 million primary carers [S64]; two-thirds of primary carers were women and 30% cared for 40 or more hours a week [S65].

Duc, 39, lost his junior software-testing job in Parramatta in 2033 when AI tools took over routine checks, the kind of loss JSA's illustrative modelling shows for programming and clerical roles while nursing grows [S25]. The floor covered his rent and food while he retrained as a nurse and the basic income replaced it in 2035. In the OpenResearch trial, recipients under 30 spent about an extra hour a week on education [S66].

Mere, 38, is a ceramic artist in Hobart. Her basic income lets her spend her best hours in the studio rather than in casual shifts. Ireland's randomised Basic Income for the Arts pilot found recipients spent about 11 more hours a week on creative work and were 15 percentage points less likely to feel depressed [S67, S68].

Graham, 71, a retired fitter in Port Augusta, lost his wife in 2041 and spends three mornings a week at the Men's Shed. His Age Pension is above the Stage 2 rate, so the basic income changes little for him.

Anna, 50, is an engineer in Brisbane who pays much more tax than before. Before the vote she paid a small levy and then the interest on a loan from the fund, and from July 2035 every income-tax rate rose by 25.5 points (Tables 6.18d and 6.7f). In return her two adult children study without needing her support, and she has a floor if AI tools take over her design work.

Tom, 58, lost his payroll job in Wollongong in 2034 when his employer automated the work, an occupation JSA's modelling places among those losing most from AI [S25]. His payment, topped up to the poverty-line floor, kept him housed but did not cover his rent comfortably [S41], and Stage 2 began only in July 2035. Retraining at 47 was slow, he pieced together casual work for three years, and the transition was hard.

Kirra, 19, is a first-year electrical apprentice in Dubbo. Her basic income tops up her apprentice wage, so she does not need a second job.

The aggregate picture

Today's data show where the gains could come from. In 2024, people who did unpaid work averaged 4 hours 25 minutes a day on it [S69], and 15% of Australians aged 15 and over were lonely [S70]. Formal volunteering fell from 36% of adults in 2010 to 25% in 2020 [S71] and 23% in 2025 on a slightly different series, and trust in others from 61.4% in 2020 to 49.9% [S62]. Figure 3.1 shows the longer volunteering series.

Mental ill-health is common and costly: in 2020–22, 21.5% of Australians aged 16 to 85 had a mental disorder in the previous year, and 38.8% of those aged 16 to 24 [S72]. The PC put its cost, with suicide, at A$200–220bn a year in 2018-19 [S73]. Trials suggest an income floor moves these numbers the right way, though modestly and not in every trial; Table 3.1 sets aims for Parliament to adopt, not forecasts.

What contributing to society looks like

In this picture contribution is broader than paid work and includes caring, volunteering, learning, making things and starting enterprises. The German pilot found recipients gave more to charity (€28 a month against €12) and more support to family and friends (€126 against €50) [S74]. In Kenya the long-term basic income raised the number of household enterprises by about 14% [S81].

The basic income stays unconditional, but two voluntary measures support objective 7 (Table 2.2). A National Contribution Register in myGov would let people record care, volunteering, learning and creative work as evidence of skills, never to test eligibility or raise debts. A Community Contribution Fund would match money raised by local groups, dollar for dollar, up to A$50,000 a project, in a pilot from 2029-30 to 2031-32 capped at A$100 million a year (Table 6.2).

Risk register

The strongest objections, answered

Objection 1. The scheme costs too much for what it delivers. If triggered, Stage 3 costs 13.2% of GDP and needs every income-tax rate 30.9 points higher; a lower-rate design modelled by Spies-Butcher, Phillips and Henderson also needed about 12 more points [S57]. A levy on market income alone cannot fund Stage 3, and under a doubled taxpayer response or the combined downside no rate rise does. Counting financing and Stage 1's own behavioural costs, its monetised net benefit in 2028-29 is −A$24.9bn to −A$4.1bn for Part 1 alone and −A$51.4bn to −A$10.7bn with the wealth-tax fund (Table 6.34). For Part 1 alone that is −A$32.5bn to −A$5.8bn with the start-up advance charged, and −A$48.3bn to −A$6.5bn a year as a level amount with the same present value to 2045-46.

The objection is right on the monetised measures and on Stage 3, which cannot be funded from income tax on the paper's central assumptions. The floor and child payment are paid for by the five durable taxes and no change to income-tax rates, with a levy from 2044-45 on central yields, or from 2032-33 if the taxes yield 80% of their costings (Table 6.14g).

Objection 2. Targeting reduces poverty more than universality does. The World Bank found existing programs about 60% more effective than a budget-neutral basic income [S105], and the OECD found one would raise poverty [S114]. In the household model the strongest targeted package, charged its own behavioural responses, lowers poverty after housing costs to 8.9% against 11.3% for Stage 1 with or without a dividend (Tables 5.4b and 5.4c).

On poverty the objection holds: no dividend reduces it, and at about the same cost the floor and child payment leave 2.4 points more poverty than the targeted package, or 2.2 points on prior-year income (Table 5.4g). On effective tax rates the two are mixed: the child payment puts far fewer higher earners at 60% or more, but second earners a higher mean participation tax rate, though none at 50% or more (Table 5.4h). A variant of Part 1 that spends its middle-income supplement on a 75% Rent Assistance rise also has less poverty (Table 5.4i). The paper keeps the supplement because a universal Stage 2 pays it to every child, at 0.32 million more people in poverty than the variant and 0.56 million to 0.61 million more than the package. Parliament can instead legislate either as Part 1 within the same framework, add its Rent Assistance rise and credit to Part 1, or choose the floor-first option.

Objection 3. People will stop working, and income should be earned. Only 38% of Australians support paying everyone the same amount regardless of work [S43]. OpenResearch found participation fell 4.2 points [S82] and hours by 1.3 a week [S83], and the Coronavirus Supplement cut job-finding by 19% [S113]. The child payment's tapers raise a second earner's participation tax rate to 38% at Stage 1, against as little as 12% today (Table 5.1b).

Most trials find small effects on work. In Germany about 90% of recipients kept working about 40 hours a week [S74], Alaska's dividend had no effect on employment [S94], and Kenya's long-term basic income did not reduce household hours [S81]. The second-earner point stands, and the taper is set by regulation so that couples' labour-supply modelling, due before the Bill, can change it (Recommendation 13). A trial fall in participation above 3 points caps Stage 2 at the floor (Table 9.6). On the costed response that happens with probability 0.86 to 0.91, so Stage 2 is not the expected outcome (Section 5).

Objection 4. The extra spending will raise prices, interest rates and rents, because it moves income from people who save to people who spend. A Mexican study found cash raised local prices compared with in-kind support [S107], and the RBA estimated that low-income households spent about 0.9 of a one-off payment on non-durables, against 0.3 to 0.7 for all households [S112]. If recipients spend 0.3 to 0.6 more of each dollar than those who pay, Stage 1 adds A$13.4bn to A$26.9bn of demand. The Section 6 scenario uses A$20bn to A$40bn, so it errs on the high side.

In the paper's scenario about 17% of Stage 1's household gains are lost to higher repayments and rents, and poverty is 11.6% to 12.0% against 11.3% first-round (Tables 6.30 and 6.31). Stage 1 is legislated well ahead so the RBA can plan, and the rent safeguard raises Rent Assistance and defers the next stage if rents outpace wages (Table 9.6).

Objection 5. Raising JobSeeker would achieve most of the gain at a fraction of the cost. ACOSS calls for at least A$600 a week [S197], and in 2023 the Business Council of Australia's chief executive backed a staged rise in JobSeeker [S138].

Stage 1 already raises the working-age payments to the poverty line, at A$19.9bn in 2028-29 with the caseload response. With automatic enrolment that is the floor-first option (A$25.0bn; poverty 12.6%), which the committee can choose. The dividend would add low-paid workers, carers and people who never claim, though for most only a little, which is why it is left to Parliament.

The red teams' objections

Tables 8.2 to 8.5 answer the third to sixth red teams [S135, S191, S192, S196], and the revision history annex records the answers to the first two and, with Table 9.3, the seventh to ninth [S198]; every paper-side error the red teams found is corrected. Across the reviews these points stand against the design:

  • the wealth tax supplies most of the wealth-tax fund's capital and all of its price, and Stage 1 without both scores better;
  • on the high reading of its losses the wealth tax raises about A$8.5bn net in present value over its life, so the stake is mostly early receipts, and those losses end the guarantee in 2041-42 and the fund in 2083-84;
  • no dividend reduces poverty, and after the taxes about a third of households are better off under every Part 2 option;
  • the floor and child payment need a levy from 2044-45 on central yields, and from 2032-33 if their taxes yield 80% of their costings, and their net benefit is negative once their own behavioural costs are counted;
  • the displacement loan bridges about a year;
  • Stage 3 cannot be certified, and on central labour evidence Stage 2 is not the expected outcome;
  • prior-year assessment still leaves some debts.

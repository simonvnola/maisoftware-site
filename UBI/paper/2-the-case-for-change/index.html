2. The case for change

Chapter 2 of 11 of the paper Beyond Work. It sets out why Australia should act now: the pace of artificial intelligence (AI), which jobs are exposed, what the safety net leaves unmet, and the objectives the paper sets. It is for readers who want the problem before the proposal.

Government should act now on the safety net, which already leaves millions in poverty, and prepare for AI, which is advancing quickly but has not yet displaced workers across the economy. That difference is why the paper proposes a targeted floor now and only tests for anything universal.

AI capability is rising quickly

One benchmark shows AI capability growing quickly and steadily. METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research), a non-profit research organisation, measures the length of software task an AI agent can complete with 50% success. That length doubled about every 7 months from 2019 to 2024 [S6], and METR's January 2026 update puts the doubling time at about 4.3 months since 2023 [S7]. These are METR's own benchmark measurements, not peer-reviewed studies (Table 2.1). Figure 2.1 plots them against each model's public release date [S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14].

The inputs to AI are growing at a similar pace: training compute for frontier models has grown about 5 times a year since 2020 [S17]. The price of running a model at fixed capability has fallen 9 to 900 times a year [S18]. Anthropic reports that its Claude Mythos preview, announced in April 2026, solved 93.9% of tasks on a standard software-engineering test, against 80.8% for Claude Opus 4.6, on its own scoring [S15, S19].

These measures have limits: the time-horizon measure covers mainly coding, a 50% success rate is not reliable unsupervised work, and models still do worse on messy real-world tasks [S20]. A randomised trial in early 2025 found experienced developers took 19% longer with AI tools [S21], although a 2026 follow-up points to modest speed-ups [S22].

Many jobs are exposed, mostly through augmentation

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimates that about 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed to AI, half likely to benefit and half to face lower demand [S23]. The International Labour Organization puts 34% of employment in high-income countries in exposed occupations [S24]. Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) finds that about 4% of the Australian workforce is in occupations highly exposed to automation and 31% to augmentation. In its illustrative modelling, clerks, receptionists and bookkeepers lose most while nurses and construction labourers gain [S25].

No aggregate displacement yet, but an early warning in entry-level hiring

There is no sign yet that AI is cutting employment across the economy. The Yale Budget Lab finds the mix of occupations in the United States not yet changing with AI [S29, S226], and Danish data rule out effects above 2% [S227]. Treasury's 2026 Intergenerational Report finds no significant Australian effects yet [S30], and most forecasts reviewed by the Productivity Commission (PC) expect a net jobs gain, though the PC calls the job effects "highly uncertain" [S32].

The early warning is in entry-level hiring. Stanford researchers find that employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed United States occupations is about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, mainly through less hiring, a pattern they call descriptive, not causal [S26, S225]. In Australia the evidence is mixed. The Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) finds that the most-exposed fifth of Australian occupations grew 5.6% from November 2022 to February 2026, against 9.5% for the least exposed, a gap that may partly reflect earlier trends [S2]. It also finds employment of 20 to 24 year olds growing slightly faster than for older workers, and the youth unemployment gap below its pre-COVID range [S2, S27]. SEEK data link AI with more early-career demand, not less [S31]. In September 2026, youth unemployment was 11.1% and overall unemployment 4.7% [S28].

This is why the paper does not ask Parliament to pay a universal income now. It asks Parliament to fix the adequacy of today's payments, which is a problem now, and to legislate the tests that would show whether AI has begun to displace work at scale.

Productivity and pay: mixed evidence

Evidence on whether technology's gains reach wages is mixed, and the paper does not rest its case on it. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) found that market-sector labour productivity rose about 65% from 1994-95 to 2021-22 while real hourly compensation rose about 47% [S33], though output per hour fell in two of the three years to 2024-25 [S34]. The PC finds the long-run gap concentrated in mining and agriculture [S35], and the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) finds labour's share of income drifting down since the 1970s [S36] (Figure 2.2).

The safety net already leaves millions in poverty

The Australian Council of Social Service (ACOSS) and the University of New South Wales (UNSW) found that 3.7 million people (14.2%) lived below the poverty line in 2022-23, up from 12.4% in 2020-21 [S37]. They included 757,000 children, or 15.6% of children under 15, the rate to which the household model in Section 6 is calibrated. Their headline line is 50% of median equivalised disposable household income, A$584 a week for a single adult in 2022-23, with poverty measured after housing costs; it is re-set each year, so it is a relative line [S38]. Poverty is highest among people relying on working-age payments: 76% for JobSeeker, 73% for Youth Allowance and 71% for Parenting Payment [S39]. The Melbourne Institute's poverty line for a single adult not in the workforce was A$569.81 a week in the March quarter of 2026 [S4], against a JobSeeker single rate of A$412.45 [S40]. In June 2024, 42% of Rent Assistance income units were in rental stress after that assistance [S41].

Public support for a response is conditional. The Lowy Institute found that 64% of Australians think AI's risks outweigh its benefits [S42]. An Australian National University (ANU) poll found 55% favoured a basic income, but only 38% backed the same payment for everyone regardless of work [S43]. No later poll was found, and the paper treats that division as a reason for a vote on each stage.

The policy problem

The policy problem is that Australia's income support ties security to paid work and means tests while AI begins to change who gets hired, with no automatic way to share AI's gains if paid work becomes scarcer. The evidence has limits: the latest poverty headcount is for 2022-23, because the ABS did not release its 2023-24 income survey [S45]. No official series has compared productivity with real pay since 2021-22, and Australia has no official AI-adjusted employment projections [S58].

Objectives

The proposal has seven numbered objectives, each with a measurable indicator, a baseline and a target date (Table 2.2). The poverty targets were set on the 22 October design's first-round rates with Stage 1 continuing, rounded up to the next half point; P* is modelled below them (10.5% for all persons and 6.6% for children in 2028-29; Table 5.7), so the recommended design meets them with more room. Objective 1(a)'s 12.0% by 2030-31 and 12.5% by 2034-35 uses the pensioner-income variant nearest Department of Social Services (DSS) data (Table 6.23). Higher rents and interest rates could put them out of reach: in the central housing scenario child poverty is 8.1%, above the 7.0% target (Table 6.32). Objective 2's targets are what the model supports, though it fits that group poorly, and objectives 3(a), 6(b), 7(a) and 7(b) apply only if Parliament later legislates a dividend (Section 10).

Why government must act

Markets will not provide this security on their own:

  1. Missing insurance. No private market insures a worker against the risk that a technology makes their skills less valuable across a whole economy, because the risk falls on many people at once and cannot be pooled.
  2. Concentrated gains. The IMF warns that "In most scenarios, AI will likely worsen overall inequality" [S23], because it lifts returns to capital. If gains concentrate, only government can share them at scale, through tax.
  3. Unpaid contribution. Carers provide about 2.2 billion hours of care a year, worth A$77.9bn [S55], and markets do not pay for this work.
  4. Failures of the current system. Means tests and mutual obligation create errors and harm; Robodebt raised about A$1.76bn in alleged debts against about 526,000 people [S56]. Stage 1 removes estimate debts from most family payments but keeps the activity test at the floor, so this failure is reduced, not removed.
  5. Coordination and timing. Payment systems take years to build, so capacity has to exist before displacement shows up in headline unemployment.

How the paper answers the questions a committee will ask

Each of the seven questions the Office of Impact Analysis sets for any policy proposal [S63] is answered in a named section (Table 2.3). The paper asks how Australia should prepare a basic income for the AI transition, through four questions of its own:

  • (a) Is there a problem that government must address? (Section 2)
  • (b) What would success look like, and what are the options? (Sections 3, 4 and 5)
  • (c) Can a basic income be funded, and who would gain and lose? (Section 6)
  • (d) How would it be implemented, consulted on, monitored and evaluated? (Sections 7, 8 and 9)

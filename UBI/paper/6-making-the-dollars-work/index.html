The recommended Stage 1 is paid for by five taxes on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel, with borrowing in its first year. It costs A$62.0bn in 2028-29, the same as Part 1, and A$1,667.6bn over 2028-29 to 2045-46, between 1.71% and 1.86% of GDP in every year (Table 6.1). In 2028-29 the five taxes fall A$20.7bn short, and the Budget borrows the gap, repaid by 30 June 2032. From 2037-38 the taxes fall short each year; surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a levy on market income from 2043-44 on central yields, or 2044-45 if Rent Assistance grows as the fiscal model's own path assumes (Table 6.6). That date is uncertain: a joint stress test gives a 48% to 74% chance of a levy before 2044-45 (Table 6.9). Every Stage 1 design has a negative monetised net benefit once the cost of the taxes is counted, and P* ranks above Part 1 on every reading (Table 6.12). A universal payment at the Age Pension rate cannot be funded from income tax on central assumptions (Tables 6.34 to 6.53). The P* figures in this chapter are provisional: they rest on the re-costing at Part 1's budget (Section 5) and on how Rent Assistance grows.

Price basis and status of these figures

All figures are the author's modelling, not a PBO costing, in nominal dollars on an underlying cash basis. They use the 2026-27 Budget's parameters: wages grow 3.5% a year, prices 2.5% and labour productivity 1.2% [S61, S127]. Nominal GDP grows 4.97% a year after 2029-30, matching the PBO's medium-term projections [S139].

The central path is now P* with no dividend, and its tables are marked "P*". Many other tables were run on the 22 October design, Part 1, often with the wealth-tax fund's dividend. Each says which design it was run on, and they are kept where the comparison or the sensitivity still applies. The dividend options' own tables are in Section 10.

As in PBO practice, the costing states its behavioural responses [S140]. Earners reduce market income by 16 cents for each dollar of net gain from the child payment, as in the OpenResearch trial [S82]. That figure is the trial's fall in income other than transfers per dollar received, not its fall in participation, so the correction of participation from 4.2 to 3.9 points does not by itself change it; it could not be re-checked against the trial's latest revision, and if it had moved in proportion it would be about 15 cents. The floor adds recipients, including people who leave work and return to payment [S113]. Taxpayers and parents respond to higher rates and tapers with a taxable-income elasticity of 0.2 (Section 11), at all incomes, not only above A$180,000 as the PBO assumes. Effects on wages, prices, employment and goods and services tax (GST) receipts are left out, and interest rates and rents enter only through a scenario.

If Rent Assistance grows instead on the fiscal model's own path (prices and population), P* costs up to A$1.5bn a year less than Part 1 by 2045-46.

The funding stack

Five durable revenue measures pay for Stage 1, each with a PBO election commitment costing (ECR) prepared for the Australian Greens at the 2025 election [S145, S146, S147, S148, S149]. Each starts in 2028-29, scaled by nominal GDP, except that the gas measure keeps falling (Tables 6.4 and 6.5). After the PBO's last year the fuel-credit and coal-and-minerals measures are held flat in real terms. The fuel-credit measure's base is machinery and large vehicles, which "will still receive fuel tax credits" as light vehicles electrify [S151]. For the minerals measure flat real growth is a conservative assumption: resource export earnings are forecast to ease on lower prices, with thermal coal volumes falling as importers "shift away from thermal coal power generation" [S152]. Growing both with GDP, as earlier drafts did, is the high case: on Part 1's run the levy then starts only in 2045-46 (Table 6.10). All five are first-round: the costings "do not include broader second round effect of this policy" [S146]. A sixth measure, a net wealth tax, appears only under one of the dividend options in Section 10, with the revenue the PBO says it would cost in personal income tax [S133, S150]; Table 6.4 shows it as a memo.

The recommended Stage 1: the five taxes, the borrowing and the levy

The five taxes do not cover P*'s first year. In 2028-29 they raise A$41.3bn against a net cost of A$62.0bn, so the Budget borrows A$20.7bn. The paper calls this what it is: borrowing, repaid with interest at the debt rate (4.13%) from the taxes' later surpluses by 30 June 2032. Net debt is A$20.7bn, A$18.8bn and A$7.7bn above baseline at 30 June 2029, 2030 and 2031. For Part 1 the borrowing was A$20.6bn.

Surpluses are then kept as debt reduction, reaching A$44.9bn at 30 June 2038. From 2037-38 the taxes fall short of net cost each year, the kept surpluses are drawn down, and on central yields a levy on market income meets the gap from 2043-44, at 0.62% of market income in 2044-45 and 2045-46 (Table 6.6; Figure 6.1). Levies total A$36.5bn to 2045-46, against A$25.8bn for Part 1, because P*'s Rent Assistance rise grows faster than Part 1's supplement. If Rent Assistance instead grows on the fiscal model's own path, the first levy is in 2044-45 and levies total A$19.8bn. The five taxes must yield 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45, against 99.7% for Part 1. The cash balance is worse than baseline in 7 of the 18 years.

A phased start to borrow less

Starting the child payment a year after the floor would avoid the borrowing altogether. Table 6.7 costs that phasing: the floor and Rent Assistance +75% from 1 July 2028, and the child payment from 1 July 2029, with families keeping current FTB in 2028-29. Stage 1 then costs A$24.8bn in 2028-29, less than the five taxes raise, so there is no borrowing; the surplus kept carries forward, and on central yields no levy is needed to 2045-46. The price is a year without the child payment, which would have paid families A$26.4bn above the Family Tax Benefit they keep (the budget saves A$37.3bn, which also includes A$7.2bn of timing, behavioural responses and the income-fall review that are not cash to families): most of Stage 1's fall in child poverty is delayed by a year. The paper offers the phased start to Parliament as an option with that price; it does not recommend it.

The risk to the funding: a joint stress test

The central levy date rests on every assumption holding at once, and they will not. A joint stress test draws them together, 4,000 times (Table 6.9). Each is drawn from a triangular distribution over the paper's own tested range: the five taxes' yield (80% to 120% of their costings), the floor's caseload response (elasticity 0.21 to 0.56), the response to the child payment's taper (0.1 to 0.4) and the labour response (0.05 to 0.29). Cost overruns of nil to A$2bn a year, the floor costed on the model's or the PBO's basis, and the household model's three calibrations are drawn too. AI displacement follows the paper's moderate or severe scenario in 10% and 5% of draws, an assumption, because the paper has no probabilities for them.

On P* the chance of a levy before 2044-45 is 48% to 74%, and before 2034-35 37% to 59%. The low ends draw only the symmetric risks (yield, behaviour and calibration); the high ends add cost overruns, the PBO's floor costing and AI displacement. The first levy is most often in 2032-33, because borrowing not repaid by 30 June 2032 is levied at once; in 22% of draws there is no levy to 2045-46. P* carries the same funding risk as Part 1 (47% to 73% before 2044-45); the targeted package carries more (70% to 87%). The 59% and 45% in the revision plan came from a first run that could not be reproduced; these figures replace them.

So the levy is better read as a range than as a date. The central path is the case in which every assumption lands on its central value at once; the range in Table 6.9 is the better guide.

Net benefit

Counting the economic cost of the taxes that finance it, every Stage 1 design and every later stage has a negative monetised net benefit (Tables 6.12 and 6.13). Payments move income between people rather than using it up, so the real costs are implementation, market income forgone and each tax's excess burden. Treasury puts that burden at 50 cents a dollar for company income tax and 21 cents for a flat tax on labour income [S159] (Table 6.11). No published estimate for a wealth tax was found [S179]; its range of 25 cents to A$1 is the author's, built from the PBO's assumed shrinking of the base by 10% of its initial value a year [S150].

The method is narrow. It counts the poverty gap closed and the cost of the taxes, and nothing else: not health, care or the value of insurance. Table 6.12 also gives each design's break-even weight: the weight at which its net benefit reaches zero.

The cash guarantee and households worse off after housing costs

No recipient is paid less under P* than under today's rules: payments are unchanged or higher, and families keep at least their current-rules FTB. The guarantee is that no one's payment falls under today's rules, not that no one loses. It does not extend to income after housing costs. In the paper's central rent and interest-rate scenario (Tables 6.31 and 6.32), mortgage rates rise 45 basis points and private renters' rents rise by the middle of the pass-through range, while pensioners' payments do not change. On that scenario 0.70 million recipient households, 16% of them, are worse off after housing costs under P*, by A$770 a year on average (Table 6.21).

Under Part 1 the figure was 1.11 million households, a quarter of recipient households, losing A$933 a year on average. P*'s Rent Assistance rise removes 37% of them. Privately renting Age Pension households fall from 84% to 25% worse off, Disability Support Pension households from 60% to 34% and Carer Payment households from 54% to 34%. Pensioners who own their home or have a mortgage do not move: 15% are worse off under both designs, because Rent Assistance does not reach them. No JobSeeker, student or Parenting Payment household is worse off under any design. The targeted package leaves 0.56 million households worse off. Most of those P* leaves worse off are Disability Support Pension households (0.28 million) and Age Pension households that own or are buying their home (0.26 million). Counting the scenario's effects, poverty on the relative line is 11.1% with P*, 11.7% with Part 1 and 9.8% with the package (Table 5.7).

Household poverty

The tables in this section were run on Part 1; P*'s results at the same budget are in Table 5.7. On the relative line that ACOSS and UNSW publish, Part 1 cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to 11.3% in the model, with or without the wealth-tax fund's dividend (Table 6.22), and P* to 10.5%. It raises the median, so the line rises from A$592 to A$631 a week: 0.93 million people are lifted and 0.16 million fall below the new line. On a line held at the pre-reform median, poverty falls to 9.6%. In the model, child poverty falls from 15.6% to 7.2% and poverty in sole-parent households from 31.9% to 16.4%. On the relative line, which rises with the median, poverty rises for groups whose incomes barely change. It goes from 11.1% to 12.1% at 67 and over, 23.3% to 24.9% in Age Pension-reliant and 63.6% to 68.2% in DSP-reliant households. For older renters it goes from 30.8% to 33.1%, for older owners from 7.8% to 8.6% and for couples without children from 11.5% to 12.2% (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-117).

That result depends on full-rate pensioners' private incomes, which the model assumes because DSS data could not be obtained [S135]. With incomes a quarter lower, the variant nearest DSS's 2015-16 data, Stage 1 gives 11.7% (11.8% without the dividend) and people aged 67 and over 13.5%; with half, 12.8% and 19.7% (Table 6.23). If triggered, Stages 2 and 3 cut poverty to 7.9% and 4.9%.

The model pays the child payment on current income, while the design pays on income 1.5 years old. A crude test draws 6% or 9% of families with children at random for a 20% or 40% fall in income, as the Reserve Bank found for about 6% of indebted households a year. It gives child poverty of 7.2% to 7.3% on the relative line and 5.5% anchored (Table 6.24). It ignores who has volatile incomes and any rises in income, so it bounds the effect rather than estimating it.

Housing tenure and interest rates

Extra demand from Stage 1 could raise interest rates and rents. The scenario was run on Part 1. The scenario assumes extra demand of 0.6% to 1.2% of GDP, more than Stage 1's household gains imply (Section 8). It also assumes a cash-rate rise of 25 to 75 basis points per 1% of GDP, anchored on the Reserve Bank's MARTIN model [S167]. That gives 15 to 90 basis points on variable mortgages [S175, S176]. Private rents capture 6.6% to 32.4% of renters' gains, the range the Australian Housing and Urban Research Institute (AHURI) found for Rent Assistance [S164], against 60% to 78% for tied allowances abroad [S165, S166].

About 17% of Stage 1's first-round household gains are then lost in the central case. Poverty after housing costs is 11.6% to 12.0% on the relative line, against 11.3% first-round, and 9.8% to 10.6% on the anchored line (Tables 6.31 and 6.32). A renting pensioner couple with no cash gain is A$582 to A$2,856 a year worse off, which is why P* raises Rent Assistance 75% and the rent safeguard raises it further (Table 9.6). Why Stage 3 is not authorised

No rate passes Stage 3's stress-tested certificate on any modelled path, so the paper describes Stage 3 and does not authorise it (Table 6.48).

  1. A levy on market income alone cannot fund it. On the headline path the most such a levy can raise in 2037-38 is A$455.0bn, at a rate of 40.4%, against a gap of A$583.3bn; above that rate, revenue falls.
  2. If taxpayers respond twice as strongly as the PBO assumes (an elasticity of 0.4), no Stage 3 rise restores funding, and at the headline rise Stage 3 is A$290.0bn short in 2045-46.
  3. In the combined downside no Stage 3 rise restores funding either: Stage 3 is A$218.4bn short in 2045-46.
  4. With wages 2% lower, no Stage 3 rise in either form restores funding, because the rate is past the revenue peak.

The same holds on the lower reference path, so Stage 3 depends on AI-driven growth far above the Budget baseline (Table 6.42) or on broader tax bases.

An AI displacement shock

In a moderate or severe AI shock the counter-cyclical rule holds the floor's and child payment's indexation once a displacement is declared, after the need test's youth unemployment leg has been met over eight consecutive quarters: 2036-37 (moderate) or 2034-35 (severe; Table 6.53). With no fund, the levy meets the gap. On Part 1's run, with no universal payment voted, it levies from 2035-36 or 2032-33 and reaches 2.37% or 6.17% of market income by 2045-46, as market income falls 7.5% or 19.4% (Tables 6.50 and 6.51). Under the wealth-tax fund option the fund would lend for about a year and the levy would reach 2.17% or 5.87%. These runs have not been repeated on P*.

Those scenarios hold GDP and the GDP-scaled taxes at baseline, so Table 6.52 adds two variants [S134]: (a) GDP falls with the lost wages, and (b) the lost wages reappear as profits taxed by company tax and the excess profits tax. With no universal payment, Part 1's levy then reaches 3.21% to 6.30% of market income by 2045-46 in the severe case and 1.43% to 2.41% in the moderate case (Table 6.52).

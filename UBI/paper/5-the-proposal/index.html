The Australian Basic Income is legislated in three stages. Stage 1 lifts working-age payments to the poverty line and replaces Family Tax Benefit with a child payment that has no cliffs, and no current recipient is worse off; the paper recommends these as Part 1. A universal dividend, Part 2, is set out as options for Parliament to choose later. Stage 2 needs a binding need test, a PBO certificate and a vote; Stage 3 is an authority with no rate, because no rate passes its certificate.

Who is eligible

The floor and child payment follow the replaced payments' residence rules. Under any Part 2 option, the dividend would go to every Australian citizen and permanent resident aged 18 and over. It would follow the newly arrived resident's waiting period that applies to JobSeeker Payment, four years for visas granted on or after 1 January 2019 [S123]. About 0.52 million adults are in that period [S124]. The child payment keeps Family Tax Benefit's one-year waiting period [S125], about 56,000 children. Temporary visa holders, including New Zealand citizens on Special Category Visas, are not eligible and pay no stage rise.

Rates and indexation

Stage 1 starts on 1 July 2028 (Table 5.1). JobSeeker, Youth Allowance, Austudy, the ABSTUDY (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander study assistance) living allowance, Parenting Payment and Special Benefit rise to a floor of A$569.81 a week for a single person, the Melbourne Institute poverty line for March 2026 [S4]. The floor keeps JobSeeker's income tests, including the partner income test, and its activity test. It adds about 150,000 recipients and A$4.9bn in 2028-29: e61's job-finding response plus a quarter of its separations response, for people who leave work and return to payment [S113]. Student payments rise 73% for the 179,000 students on living allowances and Parenting Payment 13%, which at e61's job-finding elasticity adds about 32,000 recipients and A$1.0bn (Table 6.35). Those students' allowance is A$3.0bn of the Budget's A$6.9bn student assistance line in 2028-29; the rest is loans and other spending (Table 6.35, C4a).

The child payment

The child payment supplies most of Stage 1's fall in poverty and reaches every poor family with children, at the price of income-testing 1.92 million families' payments and weaker work incentives for some parents. Each child aged 0 to 17 attracts a supplement paid to their main carer, equal to the maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB) Part A rate for a child under 13 plus the annual supplement, A$7,452 in 2028-29, indexed to prices [S128]. It replaces FTB Parts A and B (Table 5.1b). Each family receives the larger of the supplement plus its maximum FTB, withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, or the supplement alone, withdrawn at 18 cents above A$144,584. Families whose current FTB is higher keep it, and FTB's maintenance, shared-care and immunisation rules carry over.

Both schedules are continuous, so A$50 more income never lowers family payments by more than A$9, or A$19 on a second earner's own income. Payments are assessed on the latest assessed taxable income, on average 1.5 years earlier [S129], so families whose circumstances do not change cannot owe a debt from a wrong estimate. After a separation the resident parent is re-assessed at once on their own income; after a birth or job loss a family may ask for a review on an estimate, reconciled only up to what it added. About 49,500 families a year still owe a debt, averaging about A$1,000 (supplementary household_output_partf4.md, Table P11). The review costs A$0.8bn in 2028-29, rising to A$1.2bn by 2045-46 (Tables 5.1c and 6.1a).

The rule sends 22% of the child payment's cash to households in poverty, against none for a universal supplement, and all 0.49 million poor families with children gain (Table 5.1a). With a universal supplement Stage 1 would leave poverty at 14.3% on the relative line. In 2028-29 the rule costs A$5.6bn more than a universal supplement, and prior-year assessment and the review cost A$2.8bn. Parents' response to the tapers, with the payment's income effect, costs A$6.1bn, rising to A$11.8bn by 2045-46 (Table 5.1c).

Part 2: options for Parliament

Part 2 would add a yearly dividend for every eligible adult. It would not reduce poverty: Stage 1 gives 11.3% on the relative line with or without a dividend, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the line (Table 5.4b). It buys universality instead: a payment to every adult, a stake in any fund and a rail to people who lodge no tax return. Before the taxes are counted it reaches 86% of households rather than 60%; after them, about a third are better off under every option (32.8% to 33.0%; Table 5.2b).

The paper sets out four options and leaves the choice to Parliament as a later decision, after a PBO costing of each (Recommendation 4):

  • a wealth-tax fund: a Permanent AI Dividend Fund fed by the net wealth tax, the design modelled in full in Section 6;
  • a Budget-paid dividend: the same dividend paid from the five durable taxes and the levy, with no wealth tax and no fund;
  • a surplus fund: a permanent fund with no wealth tax, fed only by the durable account's surpluses;
  • no dividend: Part 1 alone.

Tables 5.2b and 5.2c compare them. With no dividend or the Budget-paid dividend the five taxes' surpluses reduce debt and are drawn before any levy, so the A$49.5bn kept meets Part 1 alone's shortfalls from 2038-39 until 2044-45; under the fund options each year's shortfall is levied (Tables 5.2b and 6.1e).

Under any option lodgers would receive the dividend when their return is assessed, and Services Australia would pay the 80% of non-lodgers assumed to enrol, an assumption with no Australian precedent: 1.22 million of 1.53 million (Tables 6.7h and 6.24e). Displaced workers lodge returns, so the rail does not reach them. The dividend is taxable, with the tax-free threshold and the Low Income Tax Offset (LITO) unchanged [S130, S131], and excluded from every test that uses adjusted taxable income, at A$10m to A$116m a year (Table 6.7i). Every adult keeps at least 53.0 cents of each dollar (Table 5.2a), so before funding no adult is worse off.

The wealth-tax fund receives the wealth tax net of the PBO's interaction line, and any surplus of the other five taxes after Stage 1's costs (Table 6.7a). It is invested as the Future Fund is, at a central return of the consumer price index (CPI) plus 4%, the bottom of that fund's mandate [S132]. Each year it pays out 4% of its average balance over three years, less 10% kept in a reserve. The Act would forbid spending the capital on the dividend and hold the dividend in cash terms until a year's levied top-up would exceed 25% of it.

The dividend would be A$47 in 2029-30, about 90 cents a week, and A$461 in 2045-46 (Table 6.7b). The wealth tax raises A$193.9bn gross to 2038-39, but the PBO's interaction line, the revenue it costs in other taxes, outlasts it [S133]. With all of that line charged, a high reading because part of it comes from a measure outside the stack, the tax's net present value is about A$8.5bn at the fund's 6.5% and negative at the debt rate (Table 6.14d). The guarantee lapses in 2041-42, after A$1.5bn of levied top-ups (Table 6.14e). The dividend is then held at A$461 to 2070-71 and falls to A$2 by 2085-86, once the line, charged to the fund's capital, has exhausted the fund in 2083-84 and the levy carries it (Table 6.14f). With three-quarters or half of the line charged to the fund, the guarantee would last to 2049-50 or 2067-68 and the fund to 2095-96 or 2113-14, and the options' ranking is unchanged (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-107).

In 2028-29 the wealth-tax fund receives A$26.6bn, almost all of it wealth tax, with a deadweight cost of A$6.6bn to A$26.4bn (Table 5.4e). Counting the taxes' economic cost and Stage 1's own behavioural costs, Part 1 alone has a net benefit of −A$24.9bn to −A$4.1bn, against −A$51.4bn to −A$10.7bn with the wealth-tax fund (Table 5.4d).

Most of the difference comes from the wealth tax rather than from paying every adult. The Budget-paid dividend pays the same dividend from the durable taxes, whose shortfalls are levied from 2040-41, A$99.8bn to 2045-46 (Table 5.4d). Over ten years it adds A$21.3bn of net cost but no deadweight cost, and on the complete accounting it breaks even at A$22 to A$65 per adult a year (Table 5.2b). The wealth-tax fund costs A$239 to A$926 per adult a year over ten years, all of it the wealth tax's price (Table 5.4f). The wealth tax buys a common asset and a lender in a displacement; without a fund, Stage 2 and 3 rises would be about 0.2 and 0.4 points higher (author's calculation from Table 6.8).

The surplus fund keeps a fund and its rules but drops the wealth tax. Fed only by the durable taxes' surpluses, it stays small, A$63.7bn in 2046, and pays A$1 in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46. It lasts at the central return, with its never-cut rule intact and a dividend of A$188 by 2085-86, but that is its best case: the rule lapses in 2089-90 at the bond rate and by 2065-66 in a fifth of 1,000 volatile return paths (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-108). Because surpluses build the fund rather than reduce debt, the levy starts in 2038-39 and totals A$77.3bn to 2045-46; the option breaks even at A$20 to A$37 per adult a year (Table 5.2b).

The no-worse-off rule

No person who receives, or would qualify for, a replaced payment will have less net income than under today's rules. The Act defines that group by the payments' income and assets tests, so older people qualify only if they meet the Age Pension tests, and counts tax-free superannuation pensions as income. The rule has four parts:

  1. A top-up: if the dividend is legislated, each recipient's payment, after the lift to the floor, is replaced only up to the dividend, and the rest continues as a means-tested top-up.
  2. An extended Recipient Protection Offset, which ensures that no protected recipient pays more tax on other income than under current rules. It is capped at the tax current rules would levy on the payment, so it does not grow with frozen thresholds; uncapped, it would cost A$18.2bn more in 2045-46 (supplementary model_output.md, Table F4-56).
  3. Family payment protection: every family keeps at least its current-rules FTB, indexed as current rules would index it. Under the proposed rule this binds for only about 5,000 families. Table 5.1c's A$3.9bn hold-harmless line is what a universal supplement would need, a step the FTB-equivalent amount largely replaces.
  4. A phase-out instead of a cliff: all tax protection, including the exemption from the Stage 2 and 3 rate rise, is withdrawn at 50 cents for each dollar of private income above a phase-out point. The phase-out counts whatever the child payment has already withdrawn, so the two together never exceed 50 cents.

The phase-out removes those cliffs but creates a protected class worth A$21,000 to A$50,000 a year to the protected worked households at Stage 3 (Table 6.27), which people may claim a payment to acquire [S134]. That response is not costed, and it would raise the cost of Stages 2 and 3. A net-income guarantee avoids both and costs A$2.9bn less in 2028-29, but protected recipients would gain little at later stages (Table 6.29); Parliament chooses at the Stage 2 vote.

Triggers for Stages 2 and 3

Each later stage needs a need test, a fiscal test and a vote, and each binds:

  1. A need test specific to AI, over the same 8 consecutive quarters. Youth unemployment must be at least 3 percentage points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations must fall at least 5% relative to the least exposed, on JSA's series. Measured as a change rather than a level since November 2022, the AI leg cannot stay met indefinitely, so a later general recession does not meet it. Productivity growth cannot start a stage.
  2. A fiscal test: the PBO costs the rise afresh and certifies that funding at least equals net cost in each of the next four years. Under the AI receipts rule, half of tax receipts above the 2026-27 Budget's projections, fixed when the Act passes and indexed to prices, count toward that funding, so faster AI-driven growth lowers the rise (Table 6.11); non-AI windfalls count too. For Stage 3 the certificate must also hold under a doubled taxpayer response and the combined downside of Section 6; no rate passes on any modelled path, so the Act legislates Stage 3 as an authority with no rate.
  3. A vote in both Houses of Parliament, with Stage 2 starting no earlier than 1 July 2032 and Stage 3 no earlier than 1 July 2037.

On the Budget's forecasts the need test is not met, so Stage 1 continues; meeting it needs the employment-to-population ratio to fall at least 2 points. A displacement is declared once the test's youth unemployment leg has been met over eight consecutive quarters, and the floor, the child payment and any dividend then keep their value. Under a fund option the gap is met from the reserve and then by a loan from the fund at the 10-year bond rate, up to a quarter of its balance, which bridges about a year until Stage 2 could start; otherwise the levy meets it (Tables 6.18b and 6.18d).

The costing's labour response comes from a trial in which participation fell 4.2 points. At that response the Australian trial trips the work-effort cap with probability 0.86 to 0.91, and Stage 2 passes with even odds only if the true fall is under 3.0 points [S82, S135]. In a displacement the expected path is therefore Stage 1 continuing, and Section 3's 2045 picture needs an Australian response under 3.0 points.

Legislating the Stage 2 and 3 authorities now lets a stage start once certified and voted, and gives the trial a rate set in law. But on the paper's own numbers they will probably not be used. A legislated contingent rise of 16 to 31 points may also change where high earners settle and when they realise income before any trigger, which is not costed.

Work incentives

The child payment's tapers weaken parents' work incentives. At 18 cents, 19% of parents with earnings face an effective marginal tax rate above 50%, against 14% today, though fewer face rates above 60% (Table 5.1b). A second earner taking a A$50,000 job keeps 62 cents of each dollar whether their partner earns A$100,000, A$130,000 or A$160,000; today they keep 61, 72 and 88 cents. The taper is set by regulation, so couples' labour-supply modelling, due by 30 June 2027, can change it; that modelling will cost second earners who stop work (Recommendation 13).

The price of universality

At the same budget, the strongest targeted package charged its own behavioural responses lowers poverty after housing costs to 8.9% on the relative line, against Stage 1's 11.3% with or without a dividend (Table 5.4c). The price of universality is therefore 2.4 points and 0.62 million people. At Part 1 alone's budget it gives 9.0%, 2.3 points and 0.61 million people. Assessed on prior-year income, as the child payment is, it gives 9.2% against Part 1's 11.4%, 10.6% against 12.2% at 67 and over, and gains for 63% of households against 60% (Table 5.4g), so Part 1 keeps 0.56 million more people in poverty on that basis.

Neither design has cliffs (Table 5.4h). The package cuts child poverty to 5.6% against 7.2% and gives the higher earner a lower mean effective marginal tax rate, 39.3% against 41.0%. The child payment puts 43.6% of higher earners at 50% or more against 21.9%, but 28.4 points of that sit at exactly 50%, its 18-cent taper on the 30% rate and the Medicare levy. Above 50% it has fewer than the package, 15.2% against 21.9%, and at 60% or more about a tenth as many, 1.5% against 14.2%. Second earners face a higher mean participation tax rate under Part 1, 32.9% against 29.0%, but none at 50% or more, against 13.5% under the package (7.0% at 60% or more). The package is stronger on poverty and household reach; on effective tax rates the result is mixed on both margins.

The paper recommends Part 1 as the base for a universal Stage 2: its child payment is the one Stages 2 and 3 pay, so a later stage changes only the adult payment. That saves at most A$0.05bn to A$0.25bn in expectation, since Stage 2 passes with probability 0.09 to 0.14 only after a qualifying displacement. Charged the same income effect as Part 1, the package's advantage is −A$0.3bn to A$4.1bn a year on the complete accounting, with 0.56 million to 0.61 million fewer people in poverty. A further alternative Part 1 is the same child payment without its supplement for families above a threshold near the median income, with the saving spent on the package's 75% Rent Assistance rise. It nearly dominates Part 1 on the paper's own measures: less poverty in every group shown, as few higher earners at high rates, a net benefit A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year higher and the same base for Stage 2, with second earners slightly worse off (Table 5.4i). The paper keeps the supplement because a universal Stage 2 pays it to every child; that choice costs 1.2 points of poverty, 0.32 million people and A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year now, for 0.6 points more families reached. The staged framework fits all three designs, so Parliament could legislate either alternative as Part 1 instead (supplementary model_output.md, Tables F4-105 and F4-113).

The paper recommends the floor and child payment as Part 1, as the base for a universal Stage 2, at the price above, and leaves Part 2 to Parliament (Table 5.3). The committee could instead choose the targeted package within the same staged framework, or the floor-first option, the cheapest way to be ready. The status quo responds to AI only through JobSeeker and existing payments, below the poverty line, with no prepared path to a universal payment (Tables 5.3 and 6.18).

Stage 1 is a targeted floor, recommended because the paper's own comparison favours it. The universal stages are a pathway whose tests are legislated now and whose rates are not. No dividend is recommended.

Stage 1: a targeted floor (P*)

P* has three parts, all from 1 July 2028 (Table 5.1). It lifts working-age payments to a floor at the poverty line. It replaces Family Tax Benefit with a child payment that has no cliffs and no supplement for middle-income families. And it raises the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance by 75%. It costs the same as Part 1 in 2028-29 on the fiscal model's basis, A$62.0bn, because the money Part 1 spent on the middle-income supplement pays for the Rent Assistance rise and a slightly smaller supplement. Its full fiscal path is in Section 6.

The variant the 22 October paper reported (A$6,973 a child, with automatic enrolment) was solved against the household model's own costing and cost A$6.2bn more than Part 1 in 2028-29 on the fiscal model's basis. This revision re-costs P* at Part 1's budget, which sets the child supplement at A$6,750. Re-costed, P* still has less poverty than Part 1 in every group, on three random seeds and three calibrations of the household model (Table 5.7). Its figures are provisional until the model's last robustness runs are checked.

Who is eligible

The floor, the child payment and Rent Assistance follow the current payments' residence rules. The child payment keeps Family Tax Benefit's one-year waiting period for new residents [S125], about 56,000 children. Temporary visa holders, including New Zealand citizens on Special Category Visas, are not eligible. Any later universal payment would follow the newly arrived resident's waiting period that applies to JobSeeker Payment, four years for visas granted on or after 1 January 2019 [S123]; about 0.52 million adults are in that period [S124].

Rates and indexation

Stage 1 starts on 1 July 2028 (Table 5.2). JobSeeker, Youth Allowance, Austudy, the ABSTUDY (Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander study assistance) living allowance, Parenting Payment and Special Benefit rise to a floor of A$569.81 a week for a single person, the Melbourne Institute poverty line for March 2026 [S4]. The floor keeps JobSeeker's income tests, including the partner income test, and its activity test. It adds about 150,000 recipients and A$4.9bn in 2028-29: e61's job-finding response plus a quarter of its separations response, for people who leave work and return to payment [S113]. Student payments rise 73% for the 179,000 students on living allowances and Parenting Payment 13%, which at e61's job-finding elasticity adds about 32,000 recipients and A$1.0bn (Table 6.55). Those students' allowance is A$3.0bn of the Budget's A$6.9bn student assistance line in 2028-29; the rest is loans and other spending (Table 6.55, C4a).

The child payment

The child payment supplies most of Stage 1's fall in child poverty, at the price of income-testing families' payments and weaker work incentives for some parents. Each child aged 0 to 17 attracts a supplement paid to their main carer, A$6,750 in 2028-29, indexed like the child supplement. It replaces FTB Parts A and B. Each family receives the supplement plus its maximum FTB, withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, until it is fully withdrawn. Families whose current FTB is higher keep it, and FTB's maintenance, shared-care and immunisation rules carry over.

P* differs from Part 1 in one respect. Part 1 also paid the supplement alone to families above that range, withdrawn at 18 cents above A$144,584, a threshold near median parental income: the middle-income supplement. That second schedule bound mainly for one-child families. P* drops it and spends the saving on the 75% Rent Assistance rise, with a slightly smaller supplement, which leaves less poverty in every group the model reports (Table 5.7).

The schedule is continuous, so A$50 more of parents' income never lowers family payments by more than A$9. Payments are assessed on the latest assessed taxable income, on average 1.5 years earlier [S129], so families whose circumstances do not change cannot owe a debt from a wrong estimate. After a separation the resident parent is re-assessed at once on their own income; after a birth or job loss a family may ask for a review on an estimate, reconciled only up to what it added. On Part 1's run about 49,500 families a year still owe a debt, averaging about A$1,000, and the review costs A$0.8bn in 2028-29, rising to A$1.2bn by 2045-46 (supplementary household_output_partf4.md, Table P11). P*'s are essentially the same: about 49,500 families and A$0.75bn in 2028-29 (provisional).

Tables 5.3 to 5.5 show the child payment as run for Part 1, whose first schedule P* keeps unchanged. They show why a universal supplement was rejected: it sends almost none of its cash to households in poverty and leaves poverty at 14.3% on the relative line (Table 5.3).

Who gains: three designs at the same budget

At the same budget, P* leaves 10.5% of people in poverty after housing costs on the relative line, against 11.3% for Part 1 and 9.0% for the targeted package charged the same income effect (Table 5.7). It has less poverty than Part 1 in every group shown: children 6.6% against 7.2%, sole-parent households 14.8% against 16.4%, people aged 67 and over 11.6% against 12.1%, and households relying on the Disability Support Pension 52.6% against 68.2%. That holds on all three random seeds and all three calibrations of the household model. Part 1 leaves 0.20 million more people in poverty.

The targeted package does better still on poverty, with 0.40 million fewer people in poverty than P* and less poverty in every group in every robustness run. It does so by adding an earned-income credit and a larger FTB Part A, which stack withdrawal rates on low- and middle-income working families, and by automatic enrolment.

Preferring P* to the package is a value judgement, and the paper states its price on every method it uses. The package leaves about 0.40 million fewer people in poverty (1.5 points). On the paper's own net-benefit method it is A$2.0bn to A$4.3bn a year ahead of P*; on the complete accounting it is between A$1.9bn a year behind and A$2.3bn ahead; with the Green Book's weights it is about A$10.7bn a year ahead. It breaks even at lower distributional weights: 1.3 to 2.4 on the paper's method and 2.0 to 3.8 on the complete accounting, against P*'s 1.5 to 3.3 and 2.3 to 4.9 (Table 6.12, provisional). On average the package also treats working families better: second earners' mean participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P*, 22.4% of second earners face 40% or more against 31.6%, and 21.8% of higher earners in families with children face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more against 41.6% (Table 5.7). P*'s work-incentive advantage is only at the extreme: 1.3% of higher earners face 60% or more under P* against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner faces 60% or more against 6.8%. The paper's second reason is the fiscal path. The package's costs grow faster, so it needs A$108.1bn of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5bn for P*, and a levy before 2044-45 is more likely, 87% against 74% in the joint stress test (Tables 6.6 and 6.9; model results Table B5; provisional). The paper judges that avoiding those very high rates and the larger levy is worth the price. Parliament may reasonably judge otherwise, and should choose the package if it weights poverty above them.

A variant of P* with automatic enrolment, re-costed to Part 1's budget, must cut the child supplement to A$5,734. It has lower overall poverty than P* (10.3%) but higher child poverty (7.0% against 6.6%), and its levies to 2045-46 are larger (A$65.4bn against A$36.5bn). It is not recommended.

Effective tax rates and second earners

P* keeps Part 1's 18-cent taper, so its effective tax rates are close to Part 1's. Of higher earners in families with children, 41.6% face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more, most of them at exactly 50% (the taper on the 30% rate, with the Medicare levy); 14.7% face more than 50% and 1.3% face 60% or more (Table 5.7). The targeted package stacks a 20-cent taper on its FTB increment and the credit's phase-out on FTB, so fewer families sit at exactly 50% but more face very high rates: 21.8% above 50% and 14.2% at 60% or more.

Second earners keep less under P*'s taper than today. Their mean participation tax rate, the share of their earnings lost to tax and withdrawn payments, is 33.0%, against 28.9% under the package, 32.9% under Part 1 and 27.3% under current rules. None faces 60% or more under P*, against 6.8% under the package. Against Part 1, P*'s rates are much the same: slightly fewer higher earners at high rates, but slightly more second earners at 40% or more (31.6% against 29.4%). So the value judgement that favours P* concerns the package, not Part 1, which P* beats on poverty. On Part 1's run, whose taper P* keeps, a second earner taking a A$50,000 job keeps 62 cents of each dollar whether their partner earns A$100,000, A$130,000 or A$160,000; today they keep 61, 72 and 88 cents (Table 5.4). The taper is set by regulation, so couples' labour-supply modelling, due by 30 June 2027, can change it; that modelling will cost second earners who stop work (Recommendation 13).

The price of choosing Part 1

The 22 October paper recommended Part 1, though its own figures favoured P* and the targeted package. This revision follows the figures. Part 1's reason was that its child payment is the one a universal Stage 2 would pay every child, so a later stage would change only the adult payment. That saves at most A$0.09bn to A$0.37bn once, in expectation, because even after a qualifying displacement Stage 2 passes the trial's work-effort cap with probability 0.16 to 0.21 at the OpenResearch trial's latest 3.9-point estimate. That saving is measured against the targeted package; against P*, which keeps the same schedule for most families, it is close to nil. The paper's method leaves out one other value of Part 1: paying the supplement to middle-income families as a universal entitlement, with its reach and payment channel. That cannot be priced; its break-even weights are in Table 6.12. Nothing the model review found lifts Part 1's advantage above about A$0.4bn once, against P*'s A$0.8bn to A$3.1bn a year.

Against that saving, at the same budget Part 1 leaves:

  • 0.20 million more people in poverty than P* (0.8 points; children 0.6 points) and A$1.5bn to A$1.8bn a year less net benefit, with more poverty in every group on every robustness run (Table 5.7);
  • 0.56 million to 0.61 million more people in poverty than the targeted package, 2.2 to 2.4 points (Tables 5.12 and 5.13).

A targeted route reaches Part 1's poverty rate for much less. The floor, automatic enrolment, Rent Assistance +75% and the earned-income credit give 11.2% for A$40.4bn, A$22.4bn a year less than Part 1's budget (Table 5.11). No value the paper can name is worth that price, so Part 1 is rejected. Parliament could still choose it, but it would need to state the value it places on paying the middle-income supplement now and accept 0.20 million more people in poverty to get it.

The cash guarantee, and who is worse off after housing costs

The 22 October paper said no current recipient would lose income. That holds only for payments. The guarantee this paper proposes is narrower and is stated as such: no one who receives, or would qualify for, a payment Stage 1 changes will be paid less than under today's rules. Under P* the only payment replaced is Family Tax Benefit, so the guarantee works through family payment protection: every family keeps at least its current-rules FTB, indexed as current rules would index it. The Act defines the protected group by the payments' income and assets tests.

The guarantee does not cover income after housing costs. Pensioners' payments do not change at Stage 1, but in the model's rent and interest-rate scenario rents and mortgage rates rise as the reform lifts other incomes. On the paper's central scenario (45 basis points on variable mortgages and the middle of the rent pass-through range), 0.70 million recipient households (1.65 million people, 16% of recipient households) are worse off after housing costs under P*, by A$770 a year on average (Table 6.21, provisional). Under Part 1 the figure was 1.11 million households (2.30 million people, 25% of recipient households), losing A$933 a year on average, including 60% of Disability Support Pension households and 84% of privately renting Age Pension households. No JobSeeker, Youth Allowance or Parenting Payment household is worse off under either design.

P*'s Rent Assistance rise removes 37% of Part 1's losers, not most of them. Privately renting Age Pension households fall from 84% to 25% worse off and Disability Support Pension households from 60% to 34%, but pensioners who own their home or have a mortgage do not move: about 15% are worse off under both designs. The rent safeguard raises Rent Assistance further if rents outpace wages (Table 9.6), but it does not reach owners.

If a later Act legislated a universal payment, the guarantee would need more parts: a Recipient Protection Offset, so that protected recipients pay no more tax on other income than under current rules, capped at the tax current rules levy on the payment; and a phase-out of protection at 50 cents a dollar above a phase-out point, so there are no cliffs. Those parts would create a protected class worth A$21,000 to A$50,000 a year to the worked households at Stage 3 (Table 6.28), which people may claim a payment to acquire [S134]. A net-income guarantee avoids that and costs less, but protected recipients would gain little at later stages (Table 6.30). The choice belongs to the Act that would set a rate.

The universal pathway: tests, not rates

The universal stages are a pathway Parliament can take if AI displaces work at scale. The Act would legislate three tests now and no rates (Table 5.15).

  1. An AI need test, over the same 8 consecutive quarters. Youth unemployment must be at least 3 percentage points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations must fall at least 5% relative to the least exposed, on JSA's series. Measured as a change since November 2022, the AI leg cannot stay met indefinitely, so a later general recession does not meet it. Productivity growth cannot start a stage. On the Budget's forecasts the test is not met; meeting it needs the employment-to-population ratio to fall at least 2 points.
  2. A randomised trial with a work-effort cap. About 5,000 working-age adults not on a payment receive a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2029 to June 2031, against a control group. If participation falls more than 3 points, with the lower end of its 90% confidence interval above 1 point, no universal payment may be proposed under the Act.
  3. A PBO certificate. The PBO costs any proposed rate afresh and certifies that funding at least equals net cost in each of the next four years; for a payment at the Age Pension rate, also under a doubled taxpayer response and the combined downside of Section 6. Under the AI receipts rule half of tax receipts above the 2026-27 Budget's projections, fixed when the Act passes and indexed to prices, count toward that funding; non-AI windfalls count too.

Only when all three are met may a Minister introduce a Bill setting a rate, and Parliament then votes on it. In a displacement declared after the need test's youth unemployment leg has been met for eight quarters, the floor and child payment keep their indexation and a levy meets any shortfall (Table 6.51).

What the pathway would cost comes from runs of the 22 October design (Part 1 with the wealth-tax fund's dividend), which have not been repeated on P*; they show the order of magnitude, not P*'s exact figures. A payment to every adult at the JobSeeker rate (Stage 2) would need every income-tax rate about 15.6 points higher on the smallest displacement that meets the need test, and 18.6 to 25.5 points on slower paths timed to the trigger (Table 6.38). The trial's own evidence makes it unlikely to start. At the trial's latest estimate of a 3.9-point fall in participation, the work-effort cap would bind with probability 0.79 to 0.84. A payment at the Age Pension rate (Stage 3) would need about 30.9 points, and no rate passes its certificate on any modelled path: a levy on market income alone cannot fund it, and under a doubled taxpayer response or the combined downside no rate rise does (Table 6.48). The paper therefore describes Stage 3 and does not authorise it.

Why legislate tests that will probably never be met? Because the tests, the data series and the trial take years to build, and the time to agree them is before a crisis, not during one. If AI cuts demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments, so targeting stops saving money and steep tapers fall on nearly everyone; a universal payment would then be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests are cheap: the trial costs about A$120m a year for two years, and the indicators and certificates about A$10m a year (Table 6.2). The pathway may never be used, and the paper says so (Section 8).

Legislating no rate also removes a risk the 22 October paper conceded: a contingent rise of 16 to 31 points written into law could change where high earners settle and when they realise income, before any trigger.

A dividend: left for later

The 22 October paper set out four ways to pay a yearly dividend to every adult and left the choice to Parliament. None reduces poverty: Stage 1 gives the same poverty rate with or without a dividend, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the line with it. This revision recommends none of them now and moves them to their own chapter, Section 10, with the tests any dividend should pass before Parliament legislates it.

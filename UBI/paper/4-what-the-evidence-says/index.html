4. What the evidence says

Basic income trials consistently show small effects on work, short-run gains in financial security and mixed effects on health, but no rich country has tested a permanent, universal, full-rate basic income. The evidence supports a modest first stage and careful testing; it does not show that a full-rate payment would work at national scale.

How to read the evidence

Each study is rated by method, sample size and relevance to Australia. A randomised controlled trial (RCT), which compares people randomly given a payment with similar people who were not, gives the strongest evidence of cause and effect. Quasi-experimental studies use a natural comparison, and observational studies show patterns without proving cause. Table 6.8 rates the fiscal and financing evidence on the same scale.

Reviews and large studies reach mixed conclusions: work effects are mostly small, but targeted programs cut poverty more per dollar. A 2020 review of 27 studies in Lancet Public Health found mixed health effects, small employment effects for men and larger ones for women with young children [S104]. A Stanford umbrella review of 16 reviews found small participation effects and better mental health, and cautioned that a fully universal program could produce quite different results [S80]. A World Bank study of 10 countries found existing programs on average about 60% more effective in reducing poverty than a budget-neutral basic income [S105], and the OECD found that a budget-neutral basic income would raise poverty [S228]. Contrary evidence and what it means for the design

The strongest evidence against a basic income concerns work effort, cost and poverty reduction per dollar. The design responds by targeting Stage 1, lifting the lowest payments first and testing before any universal step. Table 4.3 sets out each contrary finding.

OpenResearch's preregistered RCT followed 3,000 low-income United States adults aged 21 to 40 for three years, 1,000 of whom received US$1,000 a month. Recipients cut their labour-force participation by 3.9 percentage points in the trial's latest revision, and their income excluding transfers fell by about 16 cents for each dollar received [S82]; they worked 1.3 fewer hours a week [S83]. In Australia, the e61 Institute found that the Coronavirus Supplement cut recipients' chance of finding a job by 19% and raised job separations by 64% [S113]. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) found that a budget-neutral basic income in the United Kingdom would push about 7% of people into poverty and lift only 2% out, and "would not be a cost-effective way of directly reducing income poverty" [S114].

Australian precedents

The Coronavirus Supplement and Robodebt both shape the design. The Coronavirus Supplement added A$550 a fortnight to JobSeeker and related payments from 27 April to 24 September 2020, then A$250 to 31 December 2020 [S117] and A$150 until it ended on 31 March 2021 [S118]. It lifted about 470,000 Australians, including 75,000 children, out of poverty, and about 900,000 more fell into poverty as it was cut back during 2020 [S108]. ANU researchers found poverty and housing stress fell below pre-pandemic levels [S109], and a qualitative study of 92 recipients found the supplement helped people meet basic needs and look for work [S111].

Robodebt used averaged tax data to raise debts against welfare recipients until November 2019 [S119]. The Royal Commission, reporting on 7 July 2023 [S120], found the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal", as the Prime Minister quoted that day [S121]. The child payment is set by last year's income, though on Part 1's run about 49,500 families a year still owe a debt after an income-fall review (Table 5.4); P*'s figure is essentially the same. Because the floor and the child payment are still income-tested, the Act requires a human decision before any debt is raised from matched data.

What the evidence does not tell us

The trial evidence is on partial, temporary or targeted payments, which leaves gaps on permanence, economy-wide effects and the taxes that pay for a full scheme. People may behave differently when they know a payment will last for life [S122], effects on rents, wages and prices are largely unmeasured outside Kenya and Alaska [S80, S106], and no trial has measured the effect of the taxes. No trial has tested what decides the question: paying everyone in a market, raising the taxes to fund it, and keeping it going for good. Where universal cash has been paid for years from volatile revenue it has been cut or eroded: Alaska halved its dividend in 2016, and its 2025 dividend was its smallest in real terms [S99, S229].

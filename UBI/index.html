Beyond Work: a basic income for Australia's AI transition

This page sums up a policy paper that proposes a staged basic income for Australia, ready for a time when artificial intelligence (AI) displaces many workers. It is for anyone who wants the proposal, its cost and its effects in a few minutes, without reading the full paper.

What is proposed

The paper asks Parliament to legislate a three-stage Australian Basic Income (ABI). Only Stage 1 would start now. Stages 2 and 3 would each need evidence of AI job losses, a certificate from the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) and a separate vote of Parliament. The paper calls Stage 1 and its child payment "Part 1", and the optional dividend described below "Part 2". This page uses the stage numbers throughout.

Under Stage 1 no current recipient loses income. JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412 a week at the rate from 20 September 2026, the rate the paper uses, so the floor raises it by about A$158 a week. The child payment goes to families with income below A$72,631 on top of their maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB), withdrawn at 18 cents in the dollar with no cliffs. Above A$144,584, near median parental income, families keep the supplement alone, withdrawn at the same rate. The paper finds that Stage 3 cannot be funded from income tax, which is why it asks only for the authority to consider it.

What the basic income is not

These are common objections and misconceptions, answered from the paper, with two points of history added. Open each one to read the answer.

It is not communism

In communist systems the state owns farms, factories and businesses and plans what they make. This proposal changes none of that. Businesses stay privately owned, people keep their jobs and are paid by their employers, and markets still set prices.

The basic income is paid through the tax and transfer system, as JobSeeker, the Age Pension and Family Tax Benefit are now. Alaska has paid every eligible resident a yearly dividend from the earnings of its oil-funded Permanent Fund since 1982. The free-market economist Milton Friedman proposed a negative income tax, a close cousin of a basic income, in 1962, to replace other welfare programmes.

What the proposal does do is redistribute through tax. Stage 1 taxes large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel. Its revenue measures come from Parliamentary Budget Office costings prepared for the Australian Greens at the 2025 election. One Part 2 option taxes fortunes above A$100 million, and Stages 2 and 3 would need much higher income-tax rates. Whether that much redistribution is right is a fair argument about the size of government. It is a different question from who owns businesses.

It is not a basic income for everyone, starting now

Stage 1 lifts existing working-age payments to the poverty line and adds a child payment. A basic income for every adult comes only at Stage 2. That needs AI job losses to reach a legislated trigger, a PBO certificate and a vote of Parliament. For Stage 3 the paper asks only for the authority to consider it.

Parliament could also add a small yearly dividend for every adult alongside Stage 1 (Part 2). Under the wealth-tax fund option it is A$47 in 2029-30, rising to A$461 by 2045-46 (modelled estimates).

It is not expected to stop people working

Under Stage 1 the payments that rise to the floor keep the existing activity test. A Stage 2 basic income and any dividend would have no work test, so the paper relies on trial evidence and a safeguard instead.

Most trials find small effects on work. In a German pilot from 2021 to 2024, about 90% of recipients kept working about 40 hours a week, the same as people who were not paid. Alaska's dividend had no effect on employment, and Kenya's long-term basic income did not reduce household working hours.

Some evidence points the other way. In the OpenResearch trial in Texas and Illinois, from 2020 to 2023, labour-force participation among low-income adults aged 21 to 40 fell by 4.2 percentage points, and they worked 1.3 fewer hours a week.

The paper builds in an Australian trial before Stage 2. If it finds participation falling by more than 3 points, Stage 2 does not go ahead and the floor continues. At the United States trial's result that is likely: the paper puts the chance at 86% to 91%.

It does not take away other support

No current recipient loses income under Stage 1. The National Disability Insurance Scheme, Rent Assistance, the pension and energy supplements, Carer Allowance, the Carer Supplement, Paid Parental Leave and the Child Care Subsidy all continue in full. The tax-free threshold stays at A$18,200.

Family Tax Benefit is replaced by the child payment, and every family keeps at least what it would get from Family Tax Benefit under current rules.

It is not paid for by printing money, and Stage 1 needs no rise in income-tax rates

Stage 1 is paid for by five taxes and a Budget advance, then from 2044-45 a small levy on income (modelled estimate). If the five taxes raise only 80% of their costings, the levy starts in 2032-33 and peaks at 4.41% of income (modelled estimate).

The later stages are different. At the smallest AI job loss that triggers them, Stage 2 needs income-tax rates 15.6 percentage points higher and Stage 3 needs 30.9 points (modelled estimates). The paper finds Stage 3 cannot be funded from income tax.

It is not paid to everyone who lives in Australia

The floor and the child payment follow the residence rules of the payments they replace. A dividend would go to citizens and permanent residents aged 18 and over, after the same four-year waiting period that newly arrived residents face for JobSeeker. Temporary visa holders are not eligible.

It is not a prediction that AI will take every job

The paper says AI's early effects on jobs are uncertain. Stage 1's poverty effects are modelled with no AI job losses at all. If AI does displace workers, Stage 1 keeps paying and a levy meets the extra cost. The larger stages wait for evidence of displacement, and Parliament votes on each one separately.

It is not free, and it does not leave everyone better off

Stage 1 costs A$62.0 billion in 2028-29, and after the taxes 67% of households are worse off in cash in the first year (modelled estimates). Poverty rises for some groups on the relative measure, as the effect on poverty section explains.

Extra spending could also push up interest rates and rents. In the paper's scenario about 17% of Stage 1's household gains are lost that way. Poverty after housing costs is then 11.6% to 12.0% rather than 11.3%, and a renting pensioner couple with no cash gain is A$582 to A$2,856 a year worse off (modelled estimates).

The paper also accepts that Stage 1 is not the cheapest way to cut poverty. A targeted package at the same budget leaves fewer people in poverty. The paper keeps Stage 1's child payment for middle-income families because a universal Stage 2 would pay every child, and says Parliament could choose otherwise. Counting the economic cost of the taxes and their behavioural costs, Stage 1's net benefit in 2028-29 is between −A$24.9 billion and −A$4.1 billion (modelled estimate). That is a net cost, not a net gain.

Who pays for it

Five taxes pay for Stage 1: on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel. In 2028-29 they raise A$41.3 billion (modelled estimate), and a Budget advance covers the rest.

From 2038-39 the five taxes fall short of the cost each year (modelled estimate). Surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a small levy on income starts in 2044-45. If the taxes yield less than 99.7% of the costings, the levy starts earlier.

Stages 2 and 3 cost far more than the five taxes raise, so each needs a rise in income-tax rates: 15.6 percentage points for Stage 2 and 30.9 points for Stage 3 (modelled estimates). The paper's test for Stage 3 fails at that rate.

Why poverty rises for some groups under Stage 1

Stage 1 lifts working-age payments and adds a child payment. It pays nothing to people on the Age Pension or the Disability Support Pension, whose payments are already above the floor, so their incomes do not change.

The poverty line is relative. Stage 1 raises many low and middle incomes, so the median rises and the line moves from A$592 to A$631 a week (modelled estimate). People whose income did not change are measured against the higher line, and some who were just above the old line are now below it. In 2028-29 Stage 1 lifts 0.93 million people out of poverty and leaves 0.16 million newly below the raised line. No one in those groups is worse off in cash.

On a line fixed at today's median, poverty falls for every group: for all people from 14.2% to 9.6%, and for people aged 67 and over from 11.1% to 10.9% (modelled estimates). Stages 2 and 3 pay every adult, so poverty falls for the older groups too.

The size of the rise depends on how much private income full-rate pensioners have, which the author could not get from official data. With a quarter less private income, poverty among people aged 67 and over goes to 13.5% rather than 12.1%, and overall poverty to 11.7% rather than 11.3% (modelled estimates). The paper says this plainly and treats it as a cost of the design.

The paper also compares Stage 1 with a targeted package at the same budget. The targeted package has 0.56 million to 0.61 million fewer people in poverty (modelled estimate). The paper keeps the universal child payment because Stages 2 and 3 would pay every child, and says Parliament could choose otherwise.

Main risks

The paper names three key risks. Each figure is a modelled estimate.

  1. Revenue shortfall. If the five taxes yield 80% of the costings, Stage 1 alone needs a levy from 2032-33, peaking at 4.41% of income.
  2. Stronger taxpayer response. If taxpayers respond twice as strongly as the PBO assumes, no income-tax rate can fund Stage 3.
  3. Early AI displacement. In a severe shock with no fund to borrow from, Stage 1 alone needs a levy of 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46. With the wealth-tax fund it needs nil in 2034-35 and 5.87% by 2045-46.

Read the full risk register and the paper's answers to objections.

Decisions needed now

The paper asks Parliament for three decisions.

  1. Pass the ABI Act with Stage 1 by 31 December 2027, and choose a dividend option (Part 2) after PBO costings.
  2. Refer the paper to a joint select committee, reporting by 30 September 2027.
  3. Legislate the five durable taxes and yearly PBO certificates, and vote separately on each later stage.

Read the recommendations and the consultation plan.

Part 2: options for a dividend

Part 2 is not a stage. It is an optional yearly dividend paid to every adult, which Parliament could add alongside Stage 1. The paper leaves the choice to Parliament. In its model, no dividend reduces poverty, and after the taxes about a third of households gain under each option. The figures below are modelled estimates.

  • A wealth-tax fund, built from a tax on fortunes above A$100 million, pays A$461 a year by 2045-46. It costs the most and the fund may run out.
  • A Budget-paid dividend pays the same A$461 a year at a far lower price, but needs the largest levy.
  • A surplus fund pays A$1 a year in 2032-33 and A$86 a year in 2045-46.
  • Stage 1 alone, with no dividend, has the best net benefit and the smallest levy, but no universal element.

Beyond Work: preparing a basic income for Australia's AI transition

A policy paper argues that government's aim in the transition to artificial intelligence (AI) should be a good life for Australians, not jobs for their own sake. It proposes two solutions: a targeted floor for income support now, and a tested pathway to a universal payment, built before it is needed. This page sets out its argument, cost and effects for anyone who will not read the full paper.

Where Australia is now

The paper starts from three problems that exist today.

  1. The safety net leaves millions in poverty. In 2022-23, 3.7 million people (14.2%) lived below the poverty line after housing costs, up from 12.4% in 2020-21. They included 757,000 children, 15.6% of children under 15. In households that rely on JobSeeker, 76% were in poverty. A single adult on JobSeeker gets A$412.45 a week, against a poverty line of A$569.81 in the March quarter of 2026.
  2. The system ties security to paid work, through means tests that cause errors and harm. Robodebt raised about A$1.76 billion in alleged debts against about 526,000 people. In January to March 2024, 38% of Workforce Australia participants had a payment suspended under the activity test. Carers gave about 2.2 billion hours of unpaid care a year, worth A$77.9 billion in 2020, and the market pays nothing for it.
  3. Poverty costs everyone else too. On the paper's illustrative estimate, poverty costs Australia A$45.5 billion to A$205.5 billion a year in 2028-29, through lower earnings, extra services and poorer health. That is about A$23,000 a year for each person in poverty in the central case. Young people whose parents received income support are 1.8 times as likely to need it themselves.

The JobSeeker target is marked indicative because the model fits that group poorly. Higher rents and interest rates could put the targets out of reach: on the earlier design, the paper's central housing scenario puts child poverty at 8.1%, above the 7.0% target.

What is likely to happen next

Nobody knows, and the paper says so. Over the next ten years the plausible range is wide. At one end is no net loss of jobs, which most forecasts reviewed by the Productivity Commission expect. At the other, the paper's own scenarios displace 0.67 million (moderate) or 1.77 million (severe) workers by 2035-36 (modelled). The paper cannot say which is more likely, and on current evidence nobody can yet say.

  • Exposure is wide. The International Monetary Fund estimated in 2024 that about 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed to AI, half of them likely to face lower demand. The International Labour Organization put 34% of employment in high-income countries in exposed occupations in 2025. In 2025 Jobs and Skills Australia found 4% of Australian workers in occupations highly exposed to automation and 31% to augmentation.
  • There is no displacement across the economy yet. Neither Australian nor overseas data show AI cutting employment overall.
  • The early warning is in entry-level hiring. In the United States in 2026, employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most exposed jobs was about 19% below their less-exposed peers', mainly through less hiring. In Australia the evidence is mixed.
  • No one insures workers against this. No private market covers the risk that a technology makes skills less valuable across a whole economy, because it falls on many people at once. Today's system has no automatic way to share AI's gains if paid work becomes scarcer.
  • A shock would hit a system built for normal times. In the severe scenario about 437,000 workers a year would lose their jobs (modelled, provisional). That is close to the 462,000 to 510,000 JobSeeker claims granted each year over 2023-24 to 2025-26, and every one would meet the means tests first.

The lesson of the industrial revolution

In Britain's industrial revolution, output per worker rose for decades before most workers' wages followed. Many people, children among them, worked in dangerous and degrading conditions. The only floor was the Poor Law, and after 1834 the workhouse, made deliberately harsh so that people would take any work instead. Protections came late and had to be fought for.

The paper takes no figures from that period, which had none of the payment, tax and fiscal institutions Australia has now. Its lessons are qualitative. Gains do not reach most people by themselves. A floor set on punitive terms pushes people into whatever work remains, which is why the paper sets its floor at the poverty line. And protections built in the middle of a transition arrive too late for those who bear its costs.

The core idea: a good life, not jobs

The paper's thesis is that government's aim should be a good life for Australians, not jobs for their own sake. A good life here means an income that keeps people out of poverty, security against shocks they cannot insure themselves, and the time and health to care, learn and take part in their communities. Paid work is one route to that, and for most adults the main one. But it is a route, not the goal.

When the number of jobs is the goal, any job counts, and the pressure falls on the people with least choice. The paper says that where people must take whatever work is offered or lose the income they live on, it comes close to unfree labour. A floor that people can live on is what makes work a choice.

So the paper measures success by poverty, security and wellbeing. Employment is monitored, not targeted: the share of people in work is reported against Treasury's projection as a risk indicator, with no target. The trial's cap on falls in work stays, but to protect the tax base a universal payment would need, not as a jobs target.

The paper names where its own recommendation falls short. The floor keeps JobSeeker's activity test: recipients must still look for work or lose their payment, and suspensions are common. The paper keeps the test because changing it is a reform in its own right that it has not modelled or costed. It asks for a costed review of mutual obligation before the Bill (Recommendation 16).

Two solutions

The paper proposes two solutions, one for each half of the problem: a targeted floor now, and a tested pathway to a universal payment, built before it is needed.

Solution 1: a targeted floor now

Solution 1 answers the first problem: poverty now. It is Stage 1, recommended in the form the paper calls P*, and it starts on 1 July 2028. It has three parts.

  • A floor for working-age payments at the poverty line: A$569.81 a week, or A$32,570 a year in 2028-29. JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412.45 a week at the rate from 20 September 2026, so the floor raises it by about A$157 a week.
  • A child payment of A$6,750 a child a year, paid with the family's maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB). It is withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, with no cliffs.
  • A 75% rise in the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance.

In the model, P* cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to 10.5%, and child poverty from 15.6% to 6.6% (modelled, provisional). It eases the second problem only in part. The child payment has no cliffs and is assessed on past income, so families whose circumstances do not change face no debt from an income estimate. But the floor keeps the means tests and the activity test.

An effective tax rate is the share of an extra dollar of earnings lost to tax and withdrawn payments. A participation tax rate is the share a second earner loses by taking a job. A break-even weight is how many times a dollar of reduced poverty must count, against a dollar elsewhere, for the design to pay its way; lower is easier. The paper's own method uses weights of 1.5 to 2.0. Complete accounting also counts each design's effects on behaviour.

At the same cost, the package cuts poverty further and leaves 0.40 million fewer people in poverty. On average it also treats working families better: second earners' average participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P*, and fewer face high rates of 40% or 50% (modelled, provisional).

P*'s advantages are at the extreme and in the funding. Under P*, 1.3% of higher earners in families with children face an effective tax rate of 60% or more, against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner does, against 6.8%. The package needs A$108.1 billion of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5 billion, with an 87% chance of a levy before 2044-45 against 74% (modelled, provisional).

On net benefit, the package is ahead by A$2.0 billion to A$4.3 billion a year on the paper's method. On complete accounting it runs from A$1.9 billion a year behind P* to A$2.3 billion ahead. With the distributional weights in the UK Treasury's Green Book, it is about A$10.7 billion a year ahead (all modelled, provisional).

P* is the paper's choice among these designs of equal cost, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability. That is a value judgement, and the paper states its price: about 0.40 million more people in poverty, or 1.5 points. The targeted package is Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty above work incentives and fiscal risk.

Part 1 is the design the paper recommended on 22 October 2026. At the same cost it leaves 0.20 million more people in poverty than P* (modelled, provisional), so this revision rejects it. Read how the three designs compare.

Solution 2: a tested pathway to a universal payment, built before it is needed

Solution 2 answers what is likely to happen next. Nobody can yet say whether AI will displace work at scale, so the paper does not ask Parliament to set a universal payment, which would pay every adult whether or not they work. It asks for two things built before they are needed: the tests a universal payment would have to pass, with no rate, and the means to pay quickly.

The Act would legislate three tests.

  1. An AI need test. Over the same eight quarters, youth unemployment must be at least 3 points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed jobs must also fall at least 5% against the least exposed.
  2. A randomised trial. About 5,000 working-age adults would receive a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2029 to June 2031. If their labour-force participation falls by more than 3 points, no universal payment may be proposed.
  3. A certificate from the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO). The PBO must certify that funding covers the cost in each of the next four years.

The means to pay would be built now: a payment system that can reach every adult, and a surge protocol that enrols displaced workers automatically from payroll data in a declared displacement. The protocol would cost A$4.3 billion to A$11.4 billion a year by 2035-36 if it were used (modelled, provisional). That is what a surge of claims needs, even if no universal rate is ever legislated.

Only when all three tests are met could a Minister introduce a Bill setting a rate, and Parliament would then vote on it. The trial would cost about A$120 million a year for two years, and the indicators and certificates about A$10 million a year (modelled estimates).

The decisive questions

These are the questions that decide whether the recommendation stands, answered from the paper. The first three come first because they matter most. Open each one to read the answer.

Why not the targeted package?

On poverty the package is better, and on most other measures too. It leaves 9.0% of people in poverty after housing costs against 10.5% for P*, or 0.40 million fewer people (modelled, provisional). It has less poverty in every group in every robustness run.

On average the package treats working families better, and P*'s work-incentive advantage is only at the extreme. The package has fewer higher earners in families with children at effective tax rates of 50% or more: 21.8% against 41.6% under P*. But it has many more at 60% or more: 14.2% against 1.3%. Second earners keep more on average under the package, with an average participation tax rate of 28.9% against 33.0% under P* (27.3% today). Fewer of them face 40% or more (22.4% against 31.6%), but 6.8% face 60% or more, against none under P* (modelled, provisional).

The package's costs grow faster. On central tax yields it needs a levy from 2040-41 and A$108.1 billion of levies to 2045-46, against 2043-44 and A$36.5 billion for P*. The joint stress test gives it an 87% chance of a levy before 2044-45, against 74% for P* (modelled, provisional).

On net benefit, the package is ahead by A$2.0 billion to A$4.3 billion a year on the paper's method. On complete accounting it runs from A$1.9 billion behind P* to A$2.3 billion ahead, and with the UK Green Book's weights it is about A$10.7 billion ahead. It also breaks even at lower weights on cutting poverty: 1.3 to 2.4 on the paper's method and 2.0 to 3.8 on complete accounting, against 1.5 to 3.3 and 2.3 to 4.9 for P* (modelled, provisional).

Preferring P* is a value judgement, and the paper states its price on every method it uses. It judges that avoiding those very high tax rates and the larger levy is worth the price. Parliament may reasonably judge otherwise, and should choose the package if it weights poverty above them. Both designs fit the same Act.

Why legislate any universal pathway?

The paper agrees the pathway will probably not be used. There is no aggregate job loss from AI yet, and the trial's own evidence makes a universal payment unlikely to pass its tests.

The case is insurance. If AI cut demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments. Targeting would then stop saving money, and a universal payment would be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests, the data series and the trial take years to build, and it is far easier to agree them before a crisis than during one.

The premium is small: about A$120 million a year for the two trial years, and about A$10 million a year for the indicators and certificates (modelled). The pathway pays nobody and sets no rate. If the tests are never met, the money buys evidence the country would want anyway.

Who pays?

Five taxes pay for Stage 1, with borrowing in its first year and a levy on market income once the taxes and the surpluses kept from earlier years fall short. The taxes are on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel.

After those taxes, 35.4% of households are better off under P* and 64.6% are worse off (modelled, provisional). Across the paper's variants of who bears each tax, about 59% to 67% are worse off. The top tenth of households by income bears 52.9% of the taxes, but the fuel and company taxes reach lower incomes too.

The first year is borrowed: A$20.7 billion in 2028-29, repaid by 30 June 2032 (modelled, provisional). On central tax yields the levy starts in 2043-44 or 2044-45. But the joint stress test gives a 37% to 59% chance of a levy before 2034-35, with 2032-33 the most likely first year (modelled, provisional). Stage 1 needs no rise in income-tax rates, but it does need that borrowing and the levy. See what it costs and who pays.

Will people stop working?

Stage 1 pays no one regardless of work. The floor keeps JobSeeker's income tests and activity test, so it tapers away as earnings rise.

Most trials find small effects on work. A large United States trial, OpenResearch, found labour-force participation fell 3.9 percentage points. At that figure the paper's trial would stop a universal payment with probability 0.79 to 0.84 (modelled).

Second earners do keep less under P*'s child payment than today. Their average participation tax rate is 33.0% against 27.3% now (modelled, provisional). The taper is set by regulation so that modelling of couples' work, due before the Bill, can change it.

Why not just raise JobSeeker?

Stage 1 does that and more. The floor alone costs A$19.9 billion in 2028-29, including the extra people it brings onto payments (modelled). P* adds the child payment and the Rent Assistance rise because, at the same budget, they cut poverty further than any universal element.

Is the floor too low to service a mortgage?

Yes, and the paper says so. A worker displaced from a wage onto the floor, A$41,438 a year in 2035-36, cannot pay a mortgage set at the wage. The median repayment of a displaced mortgage holder is A$42,970 a year, more than the floor itself (modelled, provisional). What keeps most of them going is a partner's income.

The floor's own effect is small. It cuts the displaced mortgage holders paying more than half their remaining income in repayments from 85,000 to 63,000 in the moderate shock and from 223,000 to 168,000 in the severe one, about a quarter fewer (modelled, provisional). Buffers and lenders decide the rest: the median borrower holds over a year of repayments in offset and redraw, but in 2022 about 40% of loans held under three months, and hardship arrangements usually run three months. Displacement at scale would mean forced sales under any income-support design. What would limit them is not the rate but the rules around it: a hardship protocol with lenders, and not making people sell an investment property to qualify. The paper cannot price either and offers both as options. See what a displacement shock would do.

Will it push up rents and interest rates?

Partly. In the paper's scenario on Part 1, about 17% of Stage 1's household gains are lost to higher rents and repayments (modelled). That is why some people on payments end up worse off after housing costs, as the section on housing costs explains.

P* raises Rent Assistance by 75%. A rent safeguard in the Act would raise it further if rents grow faster than wages.

A model beyond Australia

The design is Australian, but most of its logic is not. The paper sets out what would transfer and what would not, and makes no claim about how well the floor would work elsewhere.

  • The second solution transfers most widely. Legislating tests before a shock, and building the means to pay quickly, needs real-time payroll data, an independent fiscal institution that can certify costings, and a revenue base that can carry a levy. The trial design and the rule of tests, not rates, need no Australian institution.
  • The first solution transfers most directly to systems like Australia's. Australia's main payments are flat-rate, means-tested and paid from general revenue. Where contributory unemployment insurance dominates, the floor would sit beneath it, and its rates, tests and costs would have to be worked out afresh.
  • The funding does not transfer. The five taxes rest on Australian bases, and other countries would fund a floor from their own.

The paper recommends sharing the design and its evidence through the Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) (Recommendation 17). Read what transfers and what does not.

What it costs and who pays

P* costs A$62.0 billion in 2028-29, rising to A$130.8 billion in 2045-46 (modelled, provisional). That is between 1.71% and 1.86% of gross domestic product (GDP) every year. Over 2028-29 to 2045-46 it costs A$1,667.6 billion in total.

The five taxes and the borrowing

The five taxes raise A$41.3 billion in 2028-29 (modelled). That is A$20.7 billion short of P*'s cost (modelled, provisional), and the Budget borrows the gap. The paper calls this what it is: borrowing, repaid with interest by 30 June 2032.

Starting the child payment a year after the floor would avoid the borrowing. The price is a year without the child payment, which would have paid families A$26.4 billion above the Family Tax Benefit they keep (the budget saves A$37.3 billion) (modelled, provisional). The paper offers this phased start to Parliament but does not recommend it.

The levy

From 2037-38 the five taxes fall short of the cost each year. Surpluses kept from the 2030s, up to A$44.9 billion at 30 June 2038, fill the gap for a few years (modelled, provisional). On central tax yields a levy on market income then starts in 2043-44 or 2044-45, depending on how Rent Assistance grows. It totals A$19.8 billion to A$36.5 billion to 2045-46, at no more than 0.62% of market income.

That date is uncertain. The paper's joint stress test varies tax yields, people's responses to the taxes, cost overruns and the path of AI together.

Net benefit

The paper puts a money value on Stage 1 in two ways. Its own method counts the economic cost of the taxes and values the fall in poverty at weights of 1.5 to 2.0. Complete accounting also counts each design's effects on behaviour. On complete accounting every Stage 1 design comes out negative in 2028-29 (modelled, provisional):

  • P*: −A$23.3 billion to −A$2.3 billion
  • the targeted package: −A$25.2 billion to nil
  • Part 1: −A$24.8 billion to −A$4.1 billion.

On the paper's own method, P* is −A$12.1 billion to A$3.3 billion and the package −A$10.1 billion to A$7.6 billion (modelled, provisional). The paper does not claim a positive money value for Stage 1. Its case rests on cutting poverty, weighed against work incentives and fiscal sustainability.

P* breaks even if a dollar of reduced poverty counts 1.5 to 3.3 times a dollar elsewhere on the paper's method, or 2.3 to 4.9 times on complete accounting. The package breaks even at 1.3 to 2.4 and 2.0 to 3.8 (modelled, provisional).

Paying each household its exact gap costs less than P*, because it pays only people below the line. But it takes back a dollar of payment for each dollar earned below the line, so work there pays nothing. P*'s floor and tapers avoid that. A universal payment raises the median income, and so the poverty line, by half its own amount. It must equal the whole median, A$1,181 a week for a single adult, before relative poverty ends.

Measured poverty would not stay at zero in practice. The line moves, 0.18 million people have incomes below zero after housing costs, not everyone claims, and incomes are measured with error. Read the zero-poverty costing.

What poverty costs Australia

Poverty also costs people who are not poor. It lowers earnings and taxes, adds spending on health, child protection and justice, and shortens lives. No national estimate exists, so the paper scales the only Australian one, for New South Wales, to the whole country. The sources behind it need checking.

The cost of each child is close to the lifetime cost of one year of a child's poverty. It is not cash spent on each child each year.

Poverty is passed on. Young people whose parents received income support are 1.8 times as likely to receive it themselves by age 26: 58% against 32%. More income helps children directly, but money alone does not close the whole gap. So the low case counts only half of the direct costs.

Poverty also weighs on the public health system. People in the lowest socioeconomic group carry 1.6 times the burden of disease of those in the highest. They are admitted to hospital 1.7 times as often for conditions that earlier care could have prevented. Governments spent A$15.2 billion in 2018-19 on late intervention for children and young people. Early help can cost less: quality preschool returns about A$2 for each A$1 spent. But it is not cheaper by default. An Australian trial of nurse home visiting was not yet cost-effective when the children were three.

Ending child poverty would cost less than child poverty costs. But most of that cost is lost earnings and health spread over decades, and only part of it comes back to the Budget. The paper does not claim the payments pay for themselves. It asks the Government to measure what poverty costs (Recommendation 18). Read what poverty costs.

Who is worse off after housing costs

The paper's guarantee is narrow, and it says so. No one's payment falls under today's rules. The guarantee does not protect income after housing costs.

As other incomes rise, the paper expects rents and mortgage rates to rise too. In its central scenario, 0.70 million households on current payments are worse off after housing costs under P*. That is 1.65 million people, or 16% of households on payments, losing A$770 a year on average (modelled, provisional).

Main risks

The paper names three key risks. Each figure is a modelled estimate.

  1. Revenue shortfall. The five taxes must raise 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45. The joint stress test gives a 48% to 74% chance of a levy before then (provisional).
  2. Housing. After housing costs, 0.70 million households on payments are worse off under P* (provisional). On Part 1's run, higher rents and interest rates added 0.3 to 0.7 points to poverty.
  3. Early AI displacement. In a severe shock, with no fund to borrow from, the levy reached 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46 on Part 1's run.

Read the full risk register and the paper's answers to objections.

What a displacement shock would do

The paper's main costings assume no AI job losses. It also models two displacement shocks, phased in over 2032-33 to 2035-36: a moderate one, in which 3% fewer people have jobs and wages are 3% lower, and a severe one of 8% and 8%. This revision adds what a shock would do beyond the Budget: to claims, to household debt and to house prices. The model's figures in this section are modelled estimates and provisional. The external evidence behind them was checked in the author's evidence review of 1 November 2026. The model has no data on savings or other assets, and no feedback from asset prices back into the economy.

A surge of claims

By 2035-36 the shocks add 0.95 million to 2.53 million recipients to the payments the floor lifts, or 93% to 248% of today's JobSeeker group. Over 2032-33 to 2035-36 about 164,000 (moderate) to 437,000 (severe) newly displaced workers a year would arrive, 0.7 to 2.1 times today's retrenchments. That is about a third to nine-tenths of the 462,000 to 510,000 JobSeeker claims Services Australia grants in a normal year, on top of them. In 2020 the system finalised 1.07 million claims in eight weeks.

The floor changes the rate of the payment, not the gate to it. A displaced worker still has to claim, and still faces the income tests, the assets test and a waiting period for people with savings; the paper now sources each threshold. Most displaced workers have a partner: 62% of displaced households are couples. The partner income test leaves 31% of displaced households with no payment on the floor, and 37% under today's JobSeeker rules. The shares caught by the assets test and the waiting period still cannot be computed, because the model holds no asset data.

Debt taken on at a wage

In the moderate shock, 0.35 million of the 0.66 million displaced households have a mortgage, with a median repayment of A$42,970 a year. The single floor pays A$41,438 a year in 2035-36, so no safety-net rate services such a mortgage; a partner's income is what keeps most households going. On the floor, 63,000 displaced mortgage holders in the moderate shock, and 168,000 in the severe one, would pay more than half their remaining income in repayments, against 85,000 and 223,000 on JobSeeker. About 25,000 to 67,000 of them would be likely to exhaust their buffers within three months, on the Reserve Bank's 2022 finding that about 40% of loans had under three months of buffer.

Forced sales and prices

If every one of those households sold within a year, sales would rise 11% to 30% on the 560,005 dwelling transfers the Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) recorded in 2025-26. That is a stress test, not a forecast, and it counts no investment properties, because the model has no investor households. The national price effect cannot be quantified from Australian evidence; the range of 2.9% to 31% given in an earlier draft is withdrawn. What the studies show is local: a discount on the home sold and about 1% on its neighbours in the United States, and under 1% for each extra listing nearby in western Sydney. The direction of rents is not established either, because owners who lose a home add to rental demand while displaced renters can pay less.

What the paper proposes, and what it costs

  • Automatic enrolment of displaced workers from Single Touch Payroll data, so that people who would not claim are paid: A$4.3 billion to A$11.4 billion a year by 2035-36 (modelled, provisional).
  • Waiving the liquid-assets waiting period in a declared displacement: at most A$1.6 billion to A$4.2 billion a year during the inflow (modelled, provisional).
  • A deferral of the assets test on an investment property the claimant is not required to sell, and a mortgage hardship protocol agreed in advance with the Australian Prudential Regulation Authority (APRA) and lenders. The paper cannot price either, and asks Parliament to get the evidence first.

Read the paper's analysis of second-round effects.

Decisions needed now

The paper asks Parliament for three decisions.

  1. Pass the Australian Basic Income (ABI) Act with P* as Stage 1 by 31 December 2027. If Parliament weights poverty above work incentives, pass it with the targeted package instead.
  2. Legislate the five taxes, the AI need test, the randomised trial and the PBO certificate. Legislate no universal rate and no dividend.
  3. Refer the paper to a joint select committee reporting by 30 September 2027, and ask the PBO to cost P*.

Read the recommendations and the consultation plan.

A dividend: options for later

A dividend is a yearly payment to every adult. It is separate from Stage 1 and from the pathway. The paper sets out four options and recommends none of them now.

  • A wealth-tax fund would be built from a tax on fortunes above A$100 million. It would pay A$47 a year in 2029-30 and A$461 in 2045-46. Depending on its investment return, the fund runs out between 2067-68 (at the bond rate) and 2083-84 (at the central return, on a high reading of the wealth tax's cost).
  • A Budget-paid dividend would pay the same amount from the five taxes and the levy, with no fund. Its levy would start in 2040-41.
  • A surplus fund would be built from the five taxes' surpluses. It would pay A$1 a year in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46.
  • No dividend is Stage 1 alone, which the paper recommends.

These figures are modelled estimates on the 22 October design, not rerun on P*. In the model no dividend reduces poverty, because a payment to everyone lifts the median income and the poverty line with it. After the taxes, about a third of households are better off under every option, the same as with no dividend. The paper sets tests any dividend should pass first, including a PBO costing. Read the dividend options and their tests.

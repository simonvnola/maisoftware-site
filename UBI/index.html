Beyond Work: preparing a basic income for Australia's AI transition

A policy paper recommends a targeted floor for income support now, and the tests a basic income would have to pass if artificial intelligence (AI) displaces work at scale. This page gives its recommendation, cost and effects for anyone who will not read the full paper.

What the paper recommends

The paper makes three recommendations.

  1. Fix the adequacy of income support now with a targeted floor, called Stage 1. Its design, P*, is the paper's choice among three designs of equal cost, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability.
  2. Legislate the tests a universal basic income would have to pass, so Australia can move quickly if AI displaces work at scale.
  3. Legislate no universal rate and no dividend yet. On current evidence, neither pays its way.

The paper's case for a basic income is insurance against a shock whose probability no one can yet estimate. It says plainly that the universal stages will probably never start.

Stage 1 and the designs it was compared with

Stage 1 starts on 1 July 2028. The paper calls the recommended design P*. It has three parts.

  • A floor for working-age payments at the poverty line: A$569.81 a week, or A$32,570 a year in 2028-29. JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412 a week at the rate from 20 September 2026, the rate the paper uses, so the floor raises it by about A$158 a week.
  • A child payment of A$6,750 a child a year, paid with the family's maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB). It is withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, with no cliffs.
  • A 75% rise in the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance.

The paper compared three designs at the same cost. The table shows each one in 2028-29.

An effective tax rate is the share of an extra dollar of earnings lost to tax and withdrawn payments. A participation tax rate is the share a second earner loses by taking a job. A break-even weight is how many times a dollar of reduced poverty must count, against a dollar elsewhere, for the design to pay its way; lower is easier. The paper's own method uses weights of 1.5 to 2.0. Complete accounting also counts each design's effects on behaviour.

At the same cost, the package cuts poverty further and leaves 0.40 million fewer people in poverty. On average it also treats working families better: second earners' average participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P*, and fewer face high rates of 40% or 50% (modelled, provisional).

P*'s advantages are at the extreme and in the funding. Under P*, 1.3% of higher earners in families with children face an effective tax rate of 60% or more, against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner does, against 6.8%. The package needs A$108.1 billion of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5 billion, with an 87% chance of a levy before 2044-45 against 74% (modelled, provisional).

On net benefit, the package is ahead by A$2.0 billion to A$4.3 billion a year on the paper's method. On complete accounting it runs from A$1.9 billion a year behind P* to A$2.3 billion ahead. With the distributional weights in the UK Treasury's Green Book, it is about A$10.7 billion a year ahead (all modelled, provisional).

P* is the paper's choice among these designs of equal cost, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability. That is a value judgement, and the paper states its price: about 0.40 million more people in poverty, or 1.5 points. The targeted package is Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty above work incentives and fiscal risk.

Part 1 is the design the paper recommended on 22 October 2026. At the same cost it leaves 0.20 million more people in poverty than P* (modelled, provisional), so this revision rejects it. Read how the three designs compare.

The universal pathway: tests, not rates

A universal basic income would pay every adult, whether or not they work. The paper does not ask Parliament to set one. It asks Parliament to legislate three tests that any later universal payment would have to pass.

  1. An AI need test. Over the same eight quarters, youth unemployment must be at least 3 points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed jobs must also fall at least 5% against the least exposed.
  2. A randomised trial. About 5,000 working-age adults would receive a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2029 to June 2031. If their labour-force participation falls by more than 3 points, no universal payment may be proposed.
  3. A certificate from the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO). The PBO must certify that funding covers the cost in each of the next four years.

Only when all three are met could a Minister introduce a Bill setting a rate, and Parliament would then vote on it. The trial would cost about A$120 million a year for two years, and the indicators and certificates about A$10 million a year (modelled estimates).

The decisive questions

These are the questions that decide whether the recommendation stands, answered from the paper. The first three come first because they matter most. Open each one to read the answer.

Why not the targeted package?

On poverty the package is better, and on most other measures too. It leaves 9.0% of people in poverty after housing costs against 10.5% for P*, or 0.40 million fewer people (modelled, provisional). It has less poverty in every group in every robustness run.

On average the package treats working families better, and P*'s work-incentive advantage is only at the extreme. The package has fewer higher earners in families with children at effective tax rates of 50% or more: 21.8% against 41.6% under P*. But it has many more at 60% or more: 14.2% against 1.3%. Second earners keep more on average under the package, with an average participation tax rate of 28.9% against 33.0% under P* (27.3% today). Fewer of them face 40% or more (22.4% against 31.6%), but 6.8% face 60% or more, against none under P* (modelled, provisional).

The package's costs grow faster. On central tax yields it needs a levy from 2040-41 and A$108.1 billion of levies to 2045-46, against 2043-44 and A$36.5 billion for P*. The joint stress test gives it an 87% chance of a levy before 2044-45, against 74% for P* (modelled, provisional).

On net benefit, the package is ahead by A$2.0 billion to A$4.3 billion a year on the paper's method. On complete accounting it runs from A$1.9 billion behind P* to A$2.3 billion ahead, and with the UK Green Book's weights it is about A$10.7 billion ahead. It also breaks even at lower weights on cutting poverty: 1.3 to 2.4 on the paper's method and 2.0 to 3.8 on complete accounting, against 1.5 to 3.3 and 2.3 to 4.9 for P* (modelled, provisional).

Preferring P* is a value judgement, and the paper states its price on every method it uses. It judges that avoiding those very high tax rates and the larger levy is worth the price. Parliament may reasonably judge otherwise, and should choose the package if it weights poverty above them. Both designs fit the same Act.

Why legislate any universal pathway?

The paper agrees the pathway will probably not be used. There is no aggregate job loss from AI yet, and the trial's own evidence makes a universal payment unlikely to pass its tests.

The case is insurance. If AI cut demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments. Targeting would then stop saving money, and a universal payment would be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests, the data series and the trial take years to build, and it is far easier to agree them before a crisis than during one.

The premium is small: about A$120 million a year for the two trial years, and about A$10 million a year for the indicators and certificates (modelled). The pathway pays nobody and sets no rate. If the tests are never met, the money buys evidence the country would want anyway.

Who pays?

Five taxes pay for Stage 1, with borrowing in its first year and a levy on market income once the taxes and the surpluses kept from earlier years fall short. The taxes are on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel.

After those taxes, 35.4% of households are better off under P* and 64.6% are worse off (modelled, provisional). Across the paper's variants of who bears each tax, about 59% to 67% are worse off. The top tenth of households by income bears 52.9% of the taxes, but the fuel and company taxes reach lower incomes too.

The first year is borrowed: A$20.7 billion in 2028-29, repaid by 30 June 2032 (modelled, provisional). On central tax yields the levy starts in 2043-44 or 2044-45. But the joint stress test gives a 37% to 59% chance of a levy before 2034-35, with 2032-33 the most likely first year (modelled, provisional). Stage 1 needs no rise in income-tax rates, but it does need that borrowing and the levy. See what it costs and who pays.

Will people stop working?

Stage 1 pays no one regardless of work. The floor keeps JobSeeker's income tests and activity test, so it tapers away as earnings rise.

Most trials find small effects on work. A large United States trial, OpenResearch, found labour-force participation fell 3.9 percentage points. At that figure the paper's trial would stop a universal payment with probability 0.79 to 0.84 (modelled).

Second earners do keep less under P*'s child payment than today. Their average participation tax rate is 33.0% against 27.3% now (modelled, provisional). The taper is set by regulation so that modelling of couples' work, due before the Bill, can change it.

Why not just raise JobSeeker?

Stage 1 does that and more. The floor alone costs A$19.9 billion in 2028-29, including the extra people it brings onto payments (modelled). P* adds the child payment and the Rent Assistance rise because, at the same budget, they cut poverty further than any universal element.

Will it push up rents and interest rates?

Partly. In the paper's scenario on Part 1, about 17% of Stage 1's household gains are lost to higher rents and repayments (modelled). That is why some people on payments end up worse off after housing costs, as the section on housing costs explains.

P* raises Rent Assistance by 75%. A rent safeguard in the Act would raise it further if rents grow faster than wages.

What it costs and who pays

P* costs A$62.0 billion in 2028-29, rising to A$130.8 billion in 2045-46 (modelled, provisional). That is between 1.71% and 1.86% of gross domestic product (GDP) every year. Over 2028-29 to 2045-46 it costs A$1,667.6 billion in total.

The five taxes and the borrowing

The five taxes raise A$41.3 billion in 2028-29 (modelled). That is A$20.7 billion short of P*'s cost (modelled, provisional), and the Budget borrows the gap. The paper calls this what it is: borrowing, repaid with interest by 30 June 2032.

Starting the child payment a year after the floor would avoid the borrowing. The price is a year without the child payment, which would have paid families A$26.4 billion above the Family Tax Benefit they keep (the budget saves A$37.3 billion) (modelled, provisional). The paper offers this phased start to Parliament but does not recommend it.

The levy

From 2037-38 the five taxes fall short of the cost each year. Surpluses kept from the 2030s, up to A$44.9 billion at 30 June 2038, fill the gap for a few years (modelled, provisional). On central tax yields a levy on market income then starts in 2043-44 or 2044-45, depending on how Rent Assistance grows. It totals A$19.8 billion to A$36.5 billion to 2045-46, at no more than 0.62% of market income.

That date is uncertain. The paper's joint stress test varies tax yields, people's responses to the taxes, cost overruns and the path of AI together.

Net benefit

The paper puts a money value on Stage 1 in two ways. Its own method counts the economic cost of the taxes and values the fall in poverty at weights of 1.5 to 2.0. Complete accounting also counts each design's effects on behaviour. On complete accounting every Stage 1 design comes out negative in 2028-29 (modelled, provisional):

  • P*: −A$23.3 billion to −A$2.3 billion
  • the targeted package: −A$25.2 billion to nil
  • Part 1: −A$24.8 billion to −A$4.1 billion.

On the paper's own method, P* is −A$12.1 billion to A$3.3 billion and the package −A$10.1 billion to A$7.6 billion (modelled, provisional). The paper does not claim a positive money value for Stage 1. Its case rests on cutting poverty, weighed against work incentives and fiscal sustainability.

P* breaks even if a dollar of reduced poverty counts 1.5 to 3.3 times a dollar elsewhere on the paper's method, or 2.3 to 4.9 times on complete accounting. The package breaks even at 1.3 to 2.4 and 2.0 to 3.8 (modelled, provisional).

Who is worse off after housing costs

The paper's guarantee is narrow, and it says so. No one's payment falls under today's rules. The guarantee does not protect income after housing costs.

As other incomes rise, the paper expects rents and mortgage rates to rise too. In its central scenario, 0.70 million households on current payments are worse off after housing costs under P*. That is 1.65 million people, or 16% of households on payments, losing A$770 a year on average (modelled, provisional).

Main risks

The paper names three key risks. Each figure is a modelled estimate.

  1. Revenue shortfall. The five taxes must raise 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45. The joint stress test gives a 48% to 74% chance of a levy before then (provisional).
  2. Housing. After housing costs, 0.70 million households on payments are worse off under P* (provisional). On Part 1's run, higher rents and interest rates added 0.3 to 0.7 points to poverty.
  3. Early AI displacement. In a severe shock, with no fund to borrow from, the levy reached 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46 on Part 1's run.

Decisions needed now

The paper asks Parliament for three decisions.

  1. Pass the Australian Basic Income (ABI) Act with P* as Stage 1 by 31 December 2027. If Parliament weights poverty above work incentives, pass it with the targeted package instead.
  2. Legislate the five taxes, the AI need test, the randomised trial and the PBO certificate. Legislate no universal rate and no dividend.
  3. Refer the paper to a joint select committee reporting by 30 September 2027, and ask the PBO to cost P*.

Read the recommendations and the consultation plan.

A dividend: options for later

A dividend is a yearly payment to every adult. It is separate from Stage 1 and from the pathway. The paper sets out four options and recommends none of them now.

  • A wealth-tax fund would be built from a tax on fortunes above A$100 million. It would pay A$47 a year in 2029-30 and A$461 in 2045-46. Depending on its investment return, the fund runs out between 2067-68 (at the bond rate) and 2083-84 (at the central return, on a high reading of the wealth tax's cost).
  • A Budget-paid dividend would pay the same amount from the five taxes and the levy, with no fund. Its levy would start in 2040-41.
  • A surplus fund would be built from the five taxes' surpluses. It would pay A$1 a year in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46.
  • No dividend is Stage 1 alone, which the paper recommends.

These figures are modelled estimates on the 22 October design, not rerun on P*. In the model no dividend reduces poverty, because a payment to everyone lifts the median income and the poverty line with it. After the taxes, about a third of households are better off under every option, the same as with no dividend. The paper sets tests any dividend should pass first, including a PBO costing.

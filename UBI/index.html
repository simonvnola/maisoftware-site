Beyond Work: a basic income for Australia's AI transition

This page sums up a policy paper that proposes a staged basic income for Australia, ready for a time when artificial intelligence (AI) displaces many workers. It is for anyone who wants the proposal, its cost and its effects in a few minutes, without reading the full paper.

What is proposed

The paper asks Parliament to legislate a three-stage Australian Basic Income (ABI). Only the first stage would start now. The later stages would need evidence of AI job losses, a certificate from the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) and a separate vote of Parliament.

Stage 1: Foundation Income, from 1 July 2028

Stage 1 is also called Part 1. It puts a floor under working-age payments at the poverty line: A$569.81 a week, or A$32,570 a year. JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412 a week at the rate from 20 September 2026, the rate the paper uses. No current recipient loses income under Part 1.

Part 1 also adds a child payment of A$7,452 a year for each child. Families with income below A$72,631 get it on top of their maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB), withdrawn at 18 cents in the dollar with no cliffs. Above A$144,584, which is near median parental income, families keep the supplement alone, also withdrawn at 18 cents in the dollar. Both are assessed on past income.

Stage 2: Basic Income, only if triggered

Stage 2 pays every adult at the JobSeeker rate, A$26,592 a year. It starts only if AI job losses reach a legislated trigger, the PBO certifies the funding and Parliament votes for it.

Stage 3: Full Basic Income, authority only

Stage 3 pays every adult at the Age Pension rate, A$43,471 a year. The paper asks only for the legal authority to consider it. Its modelling finds that Stage 3 cannot be funded from income tax.

What it costs

The paper's model puts the net cost of Part 1 at the figures below. The net cost is the gross cost less the payments it replaces.

Counting the economic cost of the taxes and their behavioural costs, Part 1's net benefit in 2028-29 is between −A$24.9 billion and −A$4.1 billion (modelled estimate). That is a net cost, not a net gain. If the later stages are triggered, their first-year net cost is A$335.6 billion for Stage 2 and A$685.5 billion for Stage 3 (modelled estimates).

Who pays for it

Five taxes pay for Part 1: on large superannuation balances, excess profits, mining, gas and business fuel. In 2028-29 they raise A$41.3 billion (modelled estimate), and a Budget advance covers the rest.

From 2038-39 the five taxes fall short of the cost each year (modelled estimate). Surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a small levy on income starts in 2044-45. If the taxes yield less than 99.7% of the costings, the levy starts earlier.

After the taxes, 67% of households are worse off in cash terms in the first year (modelled estimate; 30% to 70% across the paper's variants of who bears each tax).

Effect on poverty

The paper measures poverty after housing costs. In its model, Part 1 cuts poverty for the population as a whole and for children, but raises it for some groups.

Poverty also rises among couples without children and among Age Pension households. The paper says this plainly and treats it as a cost of the design.

The paper compares Part 1 with a targeted package at the same budget. The targeted package has 0.56 million to 0.61 million fewer people in poverty (modelled estimate). The paper keeps the universal child payment because a universal Stage 2 would pay every child, and says Parliament could choose otherwise.

Main risks

The paper names three key risks. Each figure is a modelled estimate.

  1. Revenue shortfall. If the five taxes yield 80% of the costings, Part 1 alone needs a levy from 2032-33, peaking at 4.41% of income.
  2. Stronger taxpayer response. If taxpayers respond twice as strongly as the PBO assumes, no income-tax rate can fund Stage 3.
  3. Early AI displacement. In a severe shock with no fund to borrow from, Part 1 alone needs a levy of 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46. With the wealth-tax fund it needs nil in 2034-35 and 5.87% by 2045-46.

Read the full risk register and the paper's answers to objections.

Decisions needed now

The paper asks Parliament for three decisions.

  1. Pass the ABI Act with Part 1 by 31 December 2027, and choose a Part 2 option after PBO costings.
  2. Refer the paper to a joint select committee, reporting by 30 September 2027.
  3. Legislate the five durable taxes and yearly PBO certificates, and vote separately on each later stage.

Read the recommendations and the consultation plan.

Part 2: options for a dividend

Part 2 is a yearly dividend paid to every adult. The paper leaves the choice to Parliament. In its model, no dividend reduces poverty, and after the taxes about a third of households gain under each option. The figures below are modelled estimates.

  • A wealth-tax fund, built from a tax on fortunes above A$100 million, pays A$461 a year by 2045-46. It costs the most and the fund may run out.
  • A Budget-paid dividend pays the same A$461 a year at a far lower price, but needs the largest levy.
  • A surplus fund pays A$1 a year in 2032-33 and A$86 a year in 2045-46.
  • Part 1 alone, with no dividend, has the best net benefit and the smallest levy, but no universal element.

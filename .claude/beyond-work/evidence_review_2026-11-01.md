# B Price response to forced or distressed sales

**B1 — INT, primary. Campbell, Giglio and Pathak, Forced Sales and House Prices, American Economic Review 101(5), August 2011, pp 2108–2131, abstract p 2108.** [Publisher DOI](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.101.5.2108) · [Author final manuscript](https://campbell.scholars.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5881/files/campbell/files/forcedsales072710.pdf) · [Earlier working paper](https://www.nber.org/papers/w14866).

Massachusetts transaction regressions estimate a **27% average foreclosure sale discount** and a nearby price reduction of **about 1% for a foreclosure 0.05 miles away**, approximately 80 metres. The abstract states: “Foreclosure discounts are on average at 27 percent of the value of a house.” It separately reports the local spillover. The working-paper abstract gives **28%**, reflecting a different version; cite the published 27%. Neither estimate is the percentage change in a national index per percentage increase in annual sales. Local estimates do not eliminate all endogeneity from weak neighbourhood conditions.

**B2 — INT, primary. Mian, Sufi and Trebbi, Foreclosures House Prices and the Real Economy, Journal of Finance 70(6), December 2015, pp 2587–2634, Table VI pp 2608–2610; interpretation p 2611; listings analysis pp 2612–2614.** [Published paper on author’s site](https://amirsufi.net/miansufitrebbi_jf_2015.pdf) · [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1111/jofi.12310).

State judicial foreclosure requirements instrument foreclosure rates. Table VI columns 1–3 estimate **−2.245, −1.751 and −1.966** for 2007–09 price growth on 2008–09 foreclosures per homeowner, with mortgage accounts as the denominator. A one-percentage-point increase in that ratio implies **1.75–2.25 percentage points lower price growth** in those specifications. This is a slope in fractions, not a log-log elasticity to foreclosure volumes. They also calculate that **10% more homes on the market corresponds to four percentage points lower prices**. This latter supply benchmark uses listings and a crisis setting, not Australian annual sales. Table VII estimates roughly **0.53 additional net listings per extra foreclosure**, illustrating crowding out of other listings. Do not assume every forced listing adds one to net market supply.

**B3 — INT, primary publication verified; exact published coefficient verified through a secondary research review. Anenberg and Kung, Estimates of the Size and Source of Price Declines Due to Nearby Foreclosures, American Economic Review 104(8), August 2014, pp 2527–2551.** [Publisher](https://doi.org/10.1257/aer.104.8.2527).

The published abstract confirms competition effects and disamenities concentrated in dense, low-price areas. The **−1.6% neighbouring sale-price effect per REO listing within 0.1 miles**, around 161 metres, is reported for four US metropolitan areas in 2007–09 in **B3b — INT, secondary: Cohen, Coughlin and Yao, Sales of Distressed Residential Property What Have We Learned from Recent Research, Federal Reserve Bank of St Louis Review 98(3), Third Quarter 2016, Table 2 p 176**. [Review](https://files.stlouisfed.org/research/publications/review/2016-09-12//sales-of-distressed-residential-property-what-have-we-learned-from-recent-research.pdf). The final article’s coefficient table was not accessible; this verification limitation should travel with the number.

The **2012 Federal Reserve working paper**, abstract p 1, instead reports **about 1%** for both foreclosure and ordinary new listings within that distance, using San Francisco only. [Earlier primary paper](https://www.federalreserve.gov/pubs/feds/2012/201284/201284pap.pdf). The figures are different versions and samples, not a confidence interval.

**B4 — AU, primary. Sijp and Li, The effect of nearby listings on house sale prices in Sydney A spatio temporal regularization approach, Real Estate Economics, first published 29 March 2026, section 3, Tables 5 and 6.** [Full text](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1111/1540-6229.70046).

The study covers seven Sydney regions, January 1995–May 2025, using a hedonic model and active-versus-prior listings design. Table 5’s Blacktown DID estimate is **−0.87% per extra concurrent listing within 250 m**, with a **95% credible interval \[−1.48%, −0.26%\]**. Several other regional DID intervals include zero; Outer South West’s point estimate is **+0.06%**. Thus the article’s broad “0.3%–0.9%” description must not become an Australian forced-sale elasticity or a universal local effect. It studies ordinary listings, not distressed sales; geographic and timing controls are central to its causal interpretation.

**B5 — AU, primary. Saunders and Tulip, A Model of the Australian Housing Market, RBA RDP 2019-01, March 2019, section 5.3 and Figure 15.** [Model responses](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2019/2019-01/model-responses.html).

The model’s rule is **2.5% lower housing costs for 1% more dwellings**, driven by new construction. Its experiment adds **18,200 dwellings**, about **0.16% of stock**, and reduces prices and rents by **0.4%**. The authors write: “This ratio (2.5) represents the inverse of the elasticity of housing demand.” A forced resale adds no dwelling to the stock. Applying this stock elasticity to either extra sales or their share of annual turnover is invalid. Sizing the AI shock requires paths for household incomes, demand, vacancies, credit, expectations and construction, not displacement counts alone.

**B6 — AU, primary. Bergmann, The Determinants of Mortgage Defaults in Australia Evidence for the Double trigger Hypothesis, RBA RDP 2020-03, July 2020, sections 3–4, Figures 5–6.** [Full paper](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/rdp/2020/2020-03/full.html).

In the **July 2015–June 2019** securitised-loan sample, most positive-equity loans in serious arrears cure or repay. Loans deeply in negative equity have **around a 50% eventual foreclosure probability** among observed resolutions. Conditional on resolving during the sample, median time after serious arrears is **three months to full repayment** and **six months to cure or foreclosure**. Repayment includes refinancing as well as selling. These are neither unemployment-to-sale durations nor an annual forced-sale probability for all stressed borrowers; unresolved cases are excluded from the duration statistic.

**RBA FSR evidence, 2020–2026.** The reviews below and the latest October 2026 review support buffers, equity and hardship as mediating channels. No checked FSR supplied a national price elasticity to forced-sale volume or a stable arrears-to-forced-sale conversion. B6 is the stronger Australian transition study; it still cannot identify voluntary distressed sales before arrears.

## What the sale scenarios support

The arithmetic is **64,000 / 560,005 × 100 = 11.43%** and **170,000 / 560,005 × 100 = 30.36%** of one year’s recorded transfers. Against the five year average, the figures are **11.73%** and **31.16%**. The supplied stress counts are scenario inputs, not observed sales or an empirically estimated sell-through rate.

**No defensible numerical national price-fall range is identified by these sources.** The requested 0.25–1.0 coefficient cannot be replaced by a verified Australian estimate. The US listings benchmark of roughly 0.4 is relevant as an explicitly imported sensitivity parameter only; applying it to annual settled/recorded sales would change its denominator and its meaning. It does not establish a lower or upper bound for Australia.

The direct competition effect is local and depends on simultaneous listings relative to local buyers. A national index effect additionally requires the location and value distribution of properties, sale timing, ordinary sellers withdrawing, investor purchases and credit availability. An AI shock may also lower purchasing power nationally and reduce local rents, so local spillovers cannot be summed into a national result. Retain 64,000 and 170,000 only as **all-sell-within-a-year stress tests**, and report price effects as unquantified pending a spatial housing model. The repayment-to-income threshold alone does not establish a sale.

## F3 Revenue sensitivity

**AU, primary. NSW Treasury, 2026–27 Budget Paper 1 Budget Statement, June 2026, Appendix E Table E.3 p E-4.** [Budget paper deposited in Parliament](https://www.parliament.nsw.gov.au/tp/files/211353/2026-27%20Budget%20Paper%20No.01%20-%20Budget%20Statement.pdf).

The 2026–27 budget effect of a **one-percentage-point increase in average residential transacted prices is A\$122m**; for **residential transaction volumes it is A\$98m**, other factors held constant. These are marginal budget sensitivities, not elasticities of national stamp-duty revenue or effects on all property taxes. A small fall can be modelled locally with opposite signs, explicitly as an inference; a large shock requires the duty schedule and transaction composition.

A forced-sale shock can lower prices while raising transactions, so these two duty channels can offset. Land tax uses taxable land and statutory valuation/exemption rules, not the residential transaction-price index. Investor sales to exempt owner-occupiers may also change the tax base. These are modelling implications, not separately estimated revenue effects. Comparable price/turnover sensitivities for every other state were not verified.

# G Rents after a price fall

**G1 — AU, primary. RBA, Do Housing Investors Pass-through Changes in Their Interest Costs to Rents, Bulletin, 17 October 2024, abstract, results and conclusion; tax data 2006–07 to 2018–19.** [Article](https://www.rba.gov.au/publications/bulletin/2024/oct/do-housing-investors-pass-through-changes-in-their-interest-costs-to-rents.html).

Investor-level tax-return regressions find small direct rent pass-through. The largest estimate translates an **A\$850 monthly interest increase into about A\$25 more monthly rent**, using the median interest change between April 2022 and January 2024 for scale. This is not an estimate of rent changes after forced sales or price falls. It supports modelling rents from housing demand, vacancies and stock, rather than asserting that landlords’ higher costs mechanically set rents.

**G2 — AU, secondary synthesis within a primary Australian research report. Eccleston, Warren, Verdouw and Flanagan, Pathways to state property tax reform, AHURI Final Report 291, November 2017, Box 4 p 36.** [Report](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/sites/default/files/migration/documents/AHURI_Final_Report_No_291_-_Pathways_to_state_property_tax_reform.pdf).

The box explains that a landlord-to-owner-occupier transfer can reduce **both rental supply and rental demand**. Quotation: “both rental supply and demand will fall.” This is a mechanism and literature synthesis, not an estimated two-year response. It assumes the purchaser’s move releases a rental dwelling or removes demand for one; household formation, vacancy, second-home purchases and geography can break a one-for-one offset.

**G3 — AU, primary descriptive research, not a causal price-fall experiment. AHURI, Affordable private rental supply and demand short-term disruption 2016–2021 and longer-term structural change 1996–2021, Final Report 416, February 2024, section 3.2.** [Report](https://www.ahuri.edu.au/sites/default/files/documents/2024-02/AHURI-Final-Report-416-Affordable-private-rental-supply-and-demand-short-term-disruption.pdf). Census analysis documents tenure changes over those periods and discusses investor sales to owner-occupiers during the pandemic, when prices were rising. It does not isolate rent movements in the two years after an asset-price fall.

**Finding:** the rental stock can shrink when an investor sells to an owner-occupier, but the dwelling remains in the overall stock and rental demand may also fall. No checked Australian causal study identifies the sign or size of rents over the following two years for the paper’s joint income-loss/forced-sale shock. Newly displaced owner-occupiers becoming renters raise rental demand; renters buying reduce it; falling household income reduces willingness to pay. Which dominates is not identified. Do not quote an automatic rent increase, rent decrease, or fixed two-year lag.

## 1. Executive summary

Australia should fix the adequacy of income support now with a targeted floor, because that cuts poverty most for the money. It should also legislate the tests a universal basic income would have to pass, so the country can move quickly if AI displaces work at scale. It should not legislate a universal rate or a dividend yet, because on current evidence neither pays its way. The case for a basic income is insurance against a shock whose probability no one can yet estimate.

After the five taxes that pay for it are counted, {{PSTAR_HH_BETTER_AFTER_FUNDING_2028_29}} of households are better off under the recommended Stage 1 in 2028-29 and {{PSTAR_HH_WORSE_AFTER_FUNDING_2028_29}} worse off; before those taxes, 60.2% gain. The losers are mostly in the top income deciles, where the taxes fall ({{PSTAR_TOP3_DECILES_SHARE_OF_LOSERS}} of households worse off are in the top three; Tables 6.14 and 6.15). Poverty after housing costs falls from 14.2% to 10.1% in the model.

The recommended Stage 1, called P\* in this paper, starts on 1 July 2028 and has three parts:

- a floor for working-age payments at the poverty line, A$569.81 a week (A$32,570 a year in 2028-29);
- a child payment of A$6,973 a child, paid with each family's maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB) and withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, with no cliffs; and
- a 75% rise in the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance.

It is recommended because the paper's own comparison favours it. At the same budget, it leaves 0.32 million fewer people in poverty than the design the 22 October paper recommended (Part 1), less poverty in every group shown, and A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year more net benefit. Part 1 paid a supplement to middle-income families so that a universal Stage 2 would later pay it to every child; that saves at most A$0.05bn to A$0.25bn in expectation, far less than its price (Table 5.7).

The targeted package is Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty above work incentives. It cuts poverty further, to about 9.0% against about 10.1% for P\*, but puts more families on high effective tax rates: 14.2% of higher earners in families with children face 60% or more, against 1.3% under P\* (Table 5.7).

Five facts weigh against the recommendation, and Parliament should know them.

1. **The Budget advance is borrowing.** The five taxes fall {{PSTAR_ADVANCE_2028_29}} short of P\*'s cost in 2028-29, and the Budget borrows the gap, repaid with interest by {{PSTAR_ADVANCE_REPAID_YEAR}} (Table 6.6). Starting the child payment a year after the floor would cut that borrowing to {{PHASED_ADVANCE_2028_29}} (Table 6.7).
2. **Every Stage 1 design has a negative net benefit on the paper's narrow method.** Counting the economic cost of the taxes and Stage 1's own behavioural costs, P\* scores {{PSTAR_NET_BENEFIT_2028_29}} a year in 2028-29, the targeted package {{PACKAGE_NET_BENEFIT_2028_29}} and Part 1 −A$24.9bn to −A$4.1bn. Part 1 ranks below P\* on every reading. The case for Stage 1 rests on cutting poverty at least cost, not on a positive monetised benefit; P\* breaks even at a distributional weight of {{PSTAR_BREAKEVEN_WEIGHT}} (Table 6.12).
3. **The guarantee covers payments, not income after housing costs.** No one's payment falls under today's rules. But after housing costs {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_HOUSEHOLDS}} recipient households ({{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_PERSONS}} people) are worse off, by {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSS_MEAN}} a year on average, mostly pensioners whose payments do not change while the poverty line and rents rise (Table 6.21).
4. **The five taxes fall short each year from the late 2030s.** Surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a levy on market income from {{PSTAR_FIRST_LEVY_CENTRAL}} on central yields. A joint stress test of the taxes' yield, behaviour, the AI path and cost overruns puts the chance of a levy before 2044-45 at {{PSTAR_LEVY_PROB_BEFORE_2044_45}} and by 2034-35 at {{PSTAR_LEVY_PROB_BY_2034_35}} (Table 6.9).

The universal stages therefore become a tested, pre-committed pathway. The Act would set the AI need test, the randomised trial and the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) certificate a universal payment must pass, and no rates. Stage 3 is described but not authorised. No dividend is recommended now; four options are set out in Section 10, each with its tests.

### The problem

AI's early effects on jobs are uncertain: there is no aggregate displacement yet, but hiring of young workers into the most exposed jobs has slowed in the United States [S2, S26, S_CANARIES, S_YALE26]. JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412 a week against a A$570 poverty line [S3, S4].

### Key risks

1. Revenue shortfall: at 80% of costings Part 1 alone needed a levy from 2032-33, peaking at 4.41% of market income; P\*'s figures are {{PSTAR_LEVY_FIRST_AT_80PC}} and {{PSTAR_LEVY_PEAK_AT_80PC}} (Table 6.10).
2. Housing: after housing costs, {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_HOUSEHOLDS}} recipient households are worse off, and higher rents and interest rates could lift poverty by 0.3 to 0.7 points (Tables 6.21 and 6.32).
3. Early AI displacement: with no fund to lend, the levy in a severe shock reached 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46 on Part 1's run (Table 6.51).

### Decisions needed now (Table 9.1)

1. Pass the ABI Act with P\* as Stage 1 by 31 December 2027, or the targeted package if Parliament weights poverty above work incentives.
2. Legislate the five durable taxes, the AI need test, the randomised trial and the PBO certificate; legislate no universal rate and no dividend.
3. Refer this paper to a joint select committee reporting by 30 September 2027, and ask the PBO to cost P\* (Recommendation 12a).

## 2. The case for change

Government should act now on the safety net, which already leaves millions in poverty, and prepare for AI, which is advancing quickly but has not yet displaced workers across the economy. That difference is why the paper proposes a targeted floor now and only tests for anything universal.

### AI capability is rising quickly

One benchmark shows AI capability growing quickly and steadily. METR (Model Evaluation and Threat Research), a non-profit research organisation, measures the length of software task an AI agent can complete with 50% success. That length doubled about every 7 months from 2019 to 2024 [S6], and METR's January 2026 update puts the doubling time at about 4.3 months since 2023 [S7]. These are METR's own benchmark measurements, not peer-reviewed studies (Table 2.1). Figure 2.1 plots them against each model's public release date [S8, S9, S10, S11, S12, S13, S14].

The inputs to AI are growing at a similar pace: training compute for frontier models has grown about 5 times a year since 2020 [S17]. The price of running a model at fixed capability has fallen 9 to 900 times a year [S18]. Anthropic reports that its Claude Mythos preview, announced in April 2026, solved 93.9% of tasks on a standard software-engineering test, against 80.8% for Claude Opus 4.6, on its own scoring [S15, S19].

These measures have limits: the time-horizon measure covers mainly coding, a 50% success rate is not reliable unsupervised work, and models still do worse on messy real-world tasks [S20]. A randomised trial in early 2025 found experienced developers took 19% longer with AI tools [S21], although a 2026 follow-up points to modest speed-ups [S22].

### Many jobs are exposed, mostly through augmentation

The International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimates that about 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed to AI, half likely to benefit and half to face lower demand [S23]. The International Labour Organization puts 34% of employment in high-income countries in exposed occupations [S24]. Jobs and Skills Australia (JSA) finds that about 4% of the Australian workforce is in occupations highly exposed to automation and 31% to augmentation. In its illustrative modelling, clerks, receptionists and bookkeepers lose most while nurses and construction labourers gain [S25].

### No aggregate displacement yet, but an early warning in entry-level hiring

There is no sign yet that AI is cutting employment across the economy. The Yale Budget Lab finds the mix of occupations in the United States not yet changing with AI [S29, S_YALE26], and Danish data rule out labour-market effects larger than 2% [S_DANISH]. Treasury's 2026 Intergenerational Report finds no significant Australian effects yet [S30], and most forecasts reviewed by the Productivity Commission (PC) expect a net jobs gain, though the PC calls the job effects "highly uncertain" [S32].

The early warning is in entry-level hiring. Stanford researchers find that employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed United States occupations is about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, mainly through less hiring, a pattern they call descriptive, not causal [S26, S_CANARIES]. In Australia the evidence is mixed. The Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) finds that the most-exposed fifth of Australian occupations grew 5.6% from November 2022 to February 2026, against 9.5% for the least exposed, a gap that may partly reflect earlier trends [S2]. It also finds employment of 20 to 24 year olds growing slightly faster than for older workers, and the youth unemployment gap below its pre-COVID range [S2, S27]. SEEK data link AI with more early-career demand, not less [S31]. In September 2026, youth unemployment was 11.1% and overall unemployment 4.7% [S28].

This is why the paper does not ask Parliament to pay a universal income now. It asks Parliament to fix the adequacy of today's payments, which is a problem now, and to legislate the tests that would show whether AI has begun to displace work at scale.

### Productivity and pay: mixed evidence

Evidence on whether technology's gains reach wages is mixed, and the paper does not rest its case on it. The Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) found that market-sector labour productivity rose about 65% from 1994-95 to 2021-22 while real hourly compensation rose about 47% [S33], though output per hour fell in two of the three years to 2024-25 [S34]. The PC finds the long-run gap concentrated in mining and agriculture [S35], and the Reserve Bank of Australia (RBA) finds labour's share of income drifting down since the 1970s [S36] (Figure 2.2).

### The safety net already leaves millions in poverty

The Australian Council of Social Service (ACOSS) and the University of New South Wales (UNSW) found that 3.7 million people (14.2%) lived below the poverty line in 2022-23, up from 12.4% in 2020-21 [S37]. They included 757,000 children, or 15.6% of children under 15, the rate to which the household model in Section 6 is calibrated. Their headline line is 50% of median equivalised disposable household income, A$584 a week for a single adult in 2022-23, with poverty measured after housing costs; it is re-set each year, so it is a relative line [S38]. Poverty is highest among people relying on working-age payments: 76% for JobSeeker, 73% for Youth Allowance and 71% for Parenting Payment [S39]. The Melbourne Institute's poverty line for a single adult not in the workforce was A$569.81 a week in the March quarter of 2026 [S4], against a JobSeeker single rate of A$412.45 [S40]. In June 2024, 42% of Rent Assistance income units were in rental stress after that assistance [S41].

Public support for a response is conditional. The Lowy Institute found that 64% of Australians think AI's risks outweigh its benefits [S42]. An Australian National University (ANU) poll found 55% favoured a basic income, but only 38% backed the same payment for everyone regardless of work [S43]. No later poll was found, and the paper treats that division as a reason for a vote on each stage.

### The policy problem

The policy problem is that Australia's income support ties security to paid work and means tests while AI begins to change who gets hired, with no automatic way to share AI's gains if paid work becomes scarcer. The evidence has limits: the latest poverty headcount is for 2022-23, because the ABS did not release its 2023-24 income survey [S45]. No official series has compared productivity with real pay since 2021-22, and Australia has no official AI-adjusted employment projections [S58].

### Objectives

The proposal has seven numbered objectives, each with a measurable indicator, a baseline and a target date (Table 2.2). The poverty targets were set on the 22 October design's first-round rates with Stage 1 continuing, rounded up to the next half point; P\* is modelled below them (10.1% for all persons and 6.1% for children in 2028-29; Table 5.7), so the recommended design meets them with more room. Objective 1(a)'s 12.0% by 2030-31 and 12.5% by 2034-35 uses the pensioner-income variant nearest Department of Social Services (DSS) data (Table 6.23). Higher rents and interest rates could put them out of reach: in the central housing scenario child poverty is 8.1%, above the 7.0% target (Table 6.32). Objective 2's targets are what the model supports, though it fits that group poorly, and objectives 3(a), 6(b), 7(a) and 7(b) apply only if Parliament later legislates a dividend (Section 10).

### Why government must act

Markets will not provide this security on their own:

1. Missing insurance. No private market insures a worker against the risk that a technology makes their skills less valuable across a whole economy, because the risk falls on many people at once and cannot be pooled.
2. Concentrated gains. The IMF warns that "In most scenarios, AI will likely worsen overall inequality" [S23], because it lifts returns to capital. If gains concentrate, only government can share them at scale, through tax.
3. Unpaid contribution. Carers provide about 2.2 billion hours of care a year, worth A$77.9bn [S55], and markets do not pay for this work.
4. Failures of the current system. Means tests and mutual obligation create errors and harm; Robodebt raised about A$1.76bn in alleged debts against about 526,000 people [S56]. Stage 1 removes estimate debts from most family payments but keeps the activity test at the floor, so this failure is reduced, not removed.
5. Coordination and timing. Payment systems take years to build, so capacity has to exist before displacement shows up in headline unemployment.

## 3. A picture of 2045

This chapter describes what a basic income could make possible by 2045. It is a picture of the pathway, not a forecast: it happens only if the pathway is triggered and every test is passed. AI must displace enough work to meet the need test, the Australian trial must find a fall in participation under 3.0 points, the PBO must certify the funding, and Parliament must then pass a new Act setting a rate and keep it. A full pension-rate basic income also needs AI-driven growth far above the Budget's baseline, and on the paper's figures it cannot be funded from income tax (Section 6). On the central path none of this happens, and Australia in 2045 has the recommended Stage 1: the floor at the poverty line, the child payment and the higher Rent Assistance. The pathway is insurance, and like most insurance it may never be used.

The seven people below are fictional and live on one path, chosen to show the pathway working. A severe AI shock from 2032-33 meets the need test early in 2035, and the floor holds its value until then. The trial has found a fall in participation under 3 points, the PBO certifies, and Parliament legislates a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2035. On the 22 October paper's run that needed a rise of every income-tax rate of 25.5 points with a dividend fund to lend, or 26.9 points without one, and there is no Stage 3 by 2045-46 (Tables 6.38 and 6.52). Duc and Tom are single, so the floor reaches them; a displaced worker whose partner earns above its cut-out would get nothing until the universal payment starts.

### Seven Australians in 2045

Four of the seven gain clearly from the universal payment, though one of them found the transition hard; two gain little, and one pays much more tax.

Leanne, 52, cares for her mother, who has dementia, for most of each day in Toowoomba. Her basic income arrives every fortnight whatever her hours, but Carer Payment is above the universal rate, so most of her income still comes as a Carer Payment top-up under the same rules. In 2022, 3.0 million Australians (11.9%) were carers and 1.2 million primary carers [S64]; two-thirds of primary carers were women and 30% cared for 40 or more hours a week [S65].

Duc, 39, lost his junior software-testing job in Parramatta in 2033 when AI tools took over routine checks, the kind of loss JSA's illustrative modelling shows for programming and clerical roles while nursing grows [S25]. The floor covered his rent and food while he retrained as a nurse and the basic income replaced it in 2035. In the OpenResearch trial, recipients under 30 spent about an extra hour a week on education [S66].

Mere, 38, is a ceramic artist in Hobart. Her basic income lets her spend her best hours in the studio rather than in casual shifts. Ireland's randomised Basic Income for the Arts pilot found recipients spent about 11 more hours a week on creative work and were 15 percentage points less likely to feel depressed [S67, S68].

Graham, 71, a retired fitter in Port Augusta, lost his wife in 2041 and spends three mornings a week at the Men's Shed. His Age Pension is above the universal rate, so the basic income changes little for him.

Anna, 50, is an engineer in Brisbane who pays much more tax than before. Before the new Act she paid a small levy, and from July 2035 every income-tax rate rose by about 25 to 27 points (Tables 6.38 and 6.52). In return her two adult children study without needing her support, and she has a floor if AI tools take over her design work.

Tom, 58, lost his payroll job in Wollongong in 2034 when his employer automated the work, an occupation JSA's modelling places among those losing most from AI [S25]. His payment, topped up to the poverty-line floor, kept him housed but did not cover his rent comfortably [S41], and the universal payment began only in July 2035. Retraining at 47 was slow, he pieced together casual work for three years, and the transition was hard.

Kirra, 19, is a first-year electrical apprentice in Dubbo. Her basic income tops up her apprentice wage, so she does not need a second job.

### What contributing to society looks like

In this picture contribution is broader than paid work and includes caring, volunteering, learning, making things and starting enterprises. The German pilot found recipients gave more to charity (€28 a month against €12) and more support to family and friends (€126 against €50) [S74]. In Kenya the long-term basic income raised the number of household enterprises by about 14% [S81].

A universal payment would stay unconditional. Two voluntary measures, which the paper proposes now, support objective 7 (Table 2.2). A National Contribution Register in myGov would let people record care, volunteering, learning and creative work as evidence of skills, never to test eligibility or raise debts. A Community Contribution Fund would match money raised by local groups, dollar for dollar, up to A$50,000 a project, in a pilot from 2029-30 to 2031-32 capped at A$100 million a year (Table 6.2).

## 4. What the evidence says

Basic income trials consistently show small effects on work, short-run gains in financial security and mixed effects on health, but no rich country has tested a permanent, universal, full-rate basic income. The evidence supports a modest first stage and careful testing; it does not show that a full-rate payment would work at national scale.

### How to read the evidence

Each study is rated by method, sample size and relevance to Australia. A randomised controlled trial (RCT), which compares people randomly given a payment with similar people who were not, gives the strongest evidence of cause and effect. Quasi-experimental studies use a natural comparison, and observational studies show patterns without proving cause. Table 6.8 rates the fiscal and financing evidence on the same scale.

### Contrary evidence and what it means for the design

The strongest evidence against a basic income concerns work effort, cost and poverty reduction per dollar. The design responds by targeting Stage 1, lifting the lowest payments first and testing before any universal step. Table 4.3 sets out each contrary finding.

OpenResearch's preregistered RCT followed 3,000 low-income United States adults aged 21 to 40 for three years, 1,000 of whom received US$1,000 a month. Recipients cut their labour-force participation by 3.9 percentage points in the trial's latest revision (4.2 in its first version), and their income excluding transfers fell by about 16 cents for each dollar received [S82]; they worked 1.3 fewer hours a week [S83]. In Australia, the e61 Institute found that the Coronavirus Supplement cut recipients' chance of finding a job by 19% and raised job separations by 64% [S113]. The Organisation for Economic Co-operation and Development (OECD) found that a budget-neutral basic income in the United Kingdom would push about 7% of people into poverty and lift only 2% out, and "would not be a cost-effective way of directly reducing income poverty" [S114].

### Australian precedents

The Coronavirus Supplement and Robodebt both shape the design. The Coronavirus Supplement added A$550 a fortnight to JobSeeker and related payments from 27 April to 24 September 2020, then A$250 to 31 December 2020 [S117] and A$150 until it ended on 31 March 2021 [S118]. It lifted about 470,000 Australians, including 75,000 children, out of poverty, and about 900,000 more fell into poverty as it was cut back during 2020 [S108]. ANU researchers found poverty and housing stress fell below pre-pandemic levels [S109], and a qualitative study of 92 recipients found the supplement helped people meet basic needs and look for work [S111].

Robodebt used averaged tax data to raise debts against welfare recipients until November 2019 [S119]. The Royal Commission, reporting on 7 July 2023 [S120], found the scheme "a crude and cruel mechanism, neither fair nor legal", as the Prime Minister quoted that day [S121]. The child payment is set by last year's income, though on Part 1's run about 49,500 families a year still owe a debt after an income-fall review (Table 5.4); P\*'s figure is {{PSTAR_RESIDUAL_DEBTS}}. Because top-ups and the floor are still income-tested, the Act requires a human decision before any debt is raised from matched data.

### What the evidence does not tell us

The trial evidence is on partial, temporary or targeted payments, which leaves gaps on permanence, economy-wide effects and the taxes that pay for a full scheme. People may behave differently when they know a payment will last for life [S122], effects on rents, wages and prices are largely unmeasured outside Kenya and Alaska [S80, S106], and no trial has measured the effect of the taxes. No trial has tested what decides the question: paying everyone in a market, raising the taxes to fund it, and keeping it going for good. Where universal cash has been paid for years from volatile revenue it has been cut or eroded: Alaska halved its dividend in 2016, and its 2025 dividend was its smallest in real terms [S99, S_ALASKA25].

## 5. The proposal

Stage 1 is a targeted floor, recommended because the paper's own comparison favours it. The universal stages are a pathway whose tests are legislated now and whose rates are not. No dividend is recommended.

### Stage 1: a targeted floor (P\*)

P\* has three parts, all from 1 July 2028 (Table 5.1). It lifts working-age payments to a floor at the poverty line. It replaces Family Tax Benefit with a child payment that has no cliffs and no supplement for middle-income families. And it raises the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance by 75%. In the household model's comparison it costs the same as Part 1, A$62.4bn, because the money Part 1 spent on the middle-income supplement pays for the Rent Assistance rise. Its full fiscal path is in Section 6.

P\*'s figures come from round 13 of the household model, on one random seed and one calibration. The model's runs on three seeds and both calibrations will replace them before the paper is lodged. If P\* weakens on those runs, the targeted package becomes the recommended Stage 1 and P\* the alternative.

### Who is eligible

The floor, the child payment and Rent Assistance follow the current payments' residence rules. The child payment keeps Family Tax Benefit's one-year waiting period for new residents [S125], about 56,000 children. Temporary visa holders, including New Zealand citizens on Special Category Visas, are not eligible. Any later universal payment would follow the newly arrived resident's waiting period that applies to JobSeeker Payment, four years for visas granted on or after 1 January 2019 [S123]; about 0.52 million adults are in that period [S124].

### The child payment

The child payment supplies most of Stage 1's fall in child poverty, at the price of income-testing families' payments and weaker work incentives for some parents. Each child aged 0 to 17 attracts a supplement paid to their main carer, A$6,973 in 2028-29, indexed to prices. It replaces FTB Parts A and B. Each family receives the supplement plus its maximum FTB, withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, until it is fully withdrawn. Families whose current FTB is higher keep it, and FTB's maintenance, shared-care and immunisation rules carry over.

P\* differs from Part 1 in one respect. Part 1 also paid the supplement alone to families above that range, withdrawn at 18 cents above A$144,584, a threshold near median parental income: the middle-income supplement. That second schedule bound mainly for one-child families. P\* drops it and spends the saving on the 75% Rent Assistance rise, which leaves less poverty in every group the model reports (Table 5.7).

The schedule is continuous, so A$50 more of parents' income never lowers family payments by more than A$9. Payments are assessed on the latest assessed taxable income, on average 1.5 years earlier [S129], so families whose circumstances do not change cannot owe a debt from a wrong estimate. After a separation the resident parent is re-assessed at once on their own income; after a birth or job loss a family may ask for a review on an estimate, reconciled only up to what it added. On Part 1's run about 49,500 families a year still owe a debt, averaging about A$1,000, and the review costs A$0.8bn in 2028-29, rising to A$1.2bn by 2045-46 (supplementary household_output_partf4.md, Table P11). P\*'s figures are {{PSTAR_RESIDUAL_DEBTS}} families and {{PSTAR_REVIEW_COST_2028_29}} in 2028-29.

### Who gains: three designs at the same budget

At the same budget, P\* leaves 10.1% of people in poverty after housing costs on the relative line, against 11.3% for Part 1 and 9.0% for the targeted package charged the same income effect (Table 5.7). It has less poverty than Part 1 in every group shown: children 6.1% against 7.2%, sole-parent households 14.0% against 16.4%, people aged 67 and over 11.6% against 12.1%, and households relying on the Disability Support Pension 51.4% against 68.2%. Because of the Rent Assistance rise it reaches 78.5% of private-renter households, against 67.4%. Part 1 reaches 0.6 points more families with children.

The targeted package does better still on poverty, with 0.28 million fewer people in poverty than P\*. It does so by adding an earned-income credit and a larger FTB Part A, which stack withdrawal rates on low- and middle-income families. Parliament should choose it if it weights poverty above work incentives.

On the paper's net-benefit method P\* beats Part 1 by A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year, and the package beats Part 1 by −A$0.3bn to A$4.1bn. In absolute terms all three are negative once the economic cost of the taxes is counted (Table 6.12).

### Effective tax rates and second earners

P\* keeps Part 1's 18-cent taper, so its effective tax rates are close to Part 1's. Of higher earners in families with children, 42.0% face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more, most of them at exactly 50% (the taper on the 30% rate, with the Medicare levy); 14.8% face more than 50% and 1.3% face 60% or more (Table 5.7). The targeted package stacks a 20-cent taper on its FTB increment and the credit's phase-out on FTB, so fewer families sit at exactly 50% but more face very high rates: 21.8% above 50% and 14.2% at 60% or more.

Second earners keep less under P\*. Their mean participation tax rate, the share of their earnings lost to tax and withdrawn payments, is 33.1%, against 28.9% under the package and 27.3% under current rules. But none faces 60% or more under P\*, against 6.8% under the package. On Part 1's run, whose taper P\* keeps, a second earner taking a A$50,000 job keeps 62 cents of each dollar whether their partner earns A$100,000, A$130,000 or A$160,000; today they keep 61, 72 and 88 cents (Table 5.4). The taper is set by regulation, so couples' labour-supply modelling, due by 30 June 2027, can change it; that modelling will cost second earners who stop work (Recommendation 13).

### The price of choosing Part 1

The 22 October paper recommended Part 1, though its own figures favoured P\* and the targeted package. This revision follows the figures. Part 1's reason was that its child payment is the one a universal Stage 2 would pay every child, so a later stage would change only the adult payment. That saves at most A$0.05bn to A$0.25bn in expectation, because Stage 2 passes with probability 0.09 to 0.14 even after a qualifying displacement (computed at the OpenResearch trial's earlier 4.2-point estimate; at 3.9 points, {{STAGE2_PASS_PROB_AT_3_9}}). A review of the model found no other benefit of Part 1 that the paper's method leaves out worth more than about {{PART1_OMITTED_BENEFIT_MAX}} a year (provisional).

Against that saving, at the same budget Part 1 leaves:

- 0.32 million more people in poverty than P\* (1.2 points; children 1.1 points) and A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn a year less net benefit, for 0.6 points more families with children gaining (Table 5.7);
- 0.56 million to 0.61 million more people in poverty than the targeted package, 2.2 to 2.4 points (Tables 5.12 and 5.13).

A targeted route reaches Part 1's poverty rate for much less. The floor, automatic enrolment, Rent Assistance +75% and the earned-income credit give 11.2% for A$40.4bn, A$22.4bn a year less than Part 1's budget (Table 5.11). No value the paper can name is worth that price, so Part 1 is rejected. Parliament could still choose it, but it would need to state the value it places on paying the middle-income supplement now and accept 0.32 million more people in poverty to get it.

### The cash guarantee, and who is worse off after housing costs

The 22 October paper said no current recipient would lose income. That holds only for payments. The guarantee this paper proposes is narrower and is stated as such: no one who receives, or would qualify for, a payment Stage 1 changes will be paid less than under today's rules. Under P\* the only payment replaced is Family Tax Benefit, so the guarantee works through family payment protection: every family keeps at least its current-rules FTB, indexed as current rules would index it. The Act defines the protected group by the payments' income and assets tests.

The guarantee does not cover income after housing costs. Pensioners' payments do not change at Stage 1, but in the model's rent scenario rents rise as the reform lifts other incomes. On that scenario {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_HOUSEHOLDS}} recipient households ({{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_PERSONS}} people, {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSER_SHARE}} of recipient households) are worse off after housing costs under P\*, by {{PSTAR_AHC_LOSS_MEAN}} a year on average (Table 6.21). On Part 1's run the provisional figure was about {{PART1_AHC_LOSER_HOUSEHOLDS}} households ({{PART1_AHC_LOSER_PERSONS}} people, about a quarter of recipient households), losing about {{PART1_AHC_LOSS_MEAN}} a year on average. They included {{PART1_AHC_LOSER_SHARE_DSP}} of Disability Support Pension households and {{PART1_AHC_LOSER_SHARE_AP_PRIVATE_RENTERS}} of privately renting Age Pension households; no JobSeeker, Youth Allowance or Parenting Payment household was worse off. P\*'s 75% Rent Assistance rise is the design's main answer, and Table 6.21 shows how much of the loss it removes. The rent safeguard raises Rent Assistance further if rents outpace wages (Table 9.6).

If a later Act legislated a universal payment, the guarantee would need more parts: a Recipient Protection Offset, so that protected recipients pay no more tax on other income than under current rules, capped at the tax current rules levy on the payment; and a phase-out of protection at 50 cents a dollar above a phase-out point, so there are no cliffs. Those parts would create a protected class worth A$21,000 to A$50,000 a year to the worked households at Stage 3 (Table 6.28), which people may claim a payment to acquire [S134]. A net-income guarantee avoids that and costs less, but protected recipients would gain little at later stages (Table 6.30). The choice belongs to the Act that would set a rate.

### The universal pathway: tests, not rates

The universal stages are a pathway Parliament can take if AI displaces work at scale. The Act would legislate three tests now and no rates (Table 5.15).

1. **An AI need test**, over the same 8 consecutive quarters. Youth unemployment must be at least 3 percentage points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations must fall at least 5% relative to the least exposed, on JSA's series. Measured as a change since November 2022, the AI leg cannot stay met indefinitely, so a later general recession does not meet it. Productivity growth cannot start a stage. On the Budget's forecasts the test is not met; meeting it needs the employment-to-population ratio to fall at least 2 points.
2. **A randomised trial with a work-effort cap.** About 5,000 working-age adults not on a payment receive a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2029 to June 2031, against a control group. If participation falls more than 3 points, with the lower end of its 90% confidence interval above 1 point, no universal payment may be proposed under the Act.
3. **A PBO certificate.** The PBO costs any proposed rate afresh and certifies that funding at least equals net cost in each of the next four years; for a payment at the Age Pension rate, also under a doubled taxpayer response and the combined downside of Section 6. Under the AI receipts rule half of tax receipts above the 2026-27 Budget's projections, fixed when the Act passes and indexed to prices, count toward that funding; non-AI windfalls count too.

Only when all three are met may a Minister introduce a Bill setting a rate, and Parliament then votes on it. In a displacement declared after the need test's youth unemployment leg has been met for eight quarters, the floor and child payment keep their indexation and a levy meets any shortfall (Table 6.51).

What the pathway would cost comes from runs of the 22 October design (Part 1 with the wealth-tax fund's dividend), which have not been repeated on P\*; they show the order of magnitude, not P\*'s exact figures. A payment to every adult at the JobSeeker rate (Stage 2) would need every income-tax rate about 15.6 points higher on the smallest displacement that meets the need test, and 18.6 to 25.5 points on slower paths timed to the trigger (Table 6.38). The trial's own evidence makes it unlikely to start. At the OpenResearch trial's earlier estimate of a 4.2-point fall in participation, the work-effort cap would bind with probability 0.86 to 0.91; at its latest estimate of 3.9 points, with probability {{WORK_CAP_ODDS_AT_3_9}}. A payment at the Age Pension rate (Stage 3) would need about 30.9 points, and no rate passes its certificate on any modelled path: a levy on market income alone cannot fund it, and under a doubled taxpayer response or the combined downside no rate rise does (Table 6.48). The paper therefore describes Stage 3 and does not authorise it.

Why legislate tests that will probably never be met? Because the tests, the data series and the trial take years to build, and the time to agree them is before a crisis, not during one. If AI cuts demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments, so targeting stops saving money and steep tapers fall on nearly everyone; a universal payment would then be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests are cheap: the trial costs about A$120m a year for two years, and the indicators and certificates about A$10m a year (Table 6.2). The pathway may never be used, and the paper says so (Section 8).

Legislating no rate also removes a risk the 22 October paper conceded: a contingent rise of 16 to 31 points written into law could change where high earners settle and when they realise income, before any trigger.

### A dividend: left for later

The 22 October paper set out four ways to pay a yearly dividend to every adult and left the choice to Parliament. None reduces poverty: Stage 1 gives the same poverty rate with or without a dividend, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the line with it. This revision recommends none of them now and moves them to their own chapter, Section 10, with the tests any dividend should pass before Parliament legislates it.

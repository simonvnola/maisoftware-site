### No aggregate displacement yet, but an early warning in entry-level hiring

There is no sign yet that AI is cutting employment across the economy. The Yale Budget Lab finds the mix of occupations in the United States not yet changing with AI [S29, S_YALE26], and Danish data rule out effects above 2% [S_DANISH]. Treasury's 2026 Intergenerational Report finds no significant Australian effects yet [S30], and most forecasts reviewed by the Productivity Commission (PC) expect a net jobs gain, though the PC calls the job effects "highly uncertain" [S32].

The early warning is in entry-level hiring. Stanford researchers find that employment of 22 to 25 year olds in the most AI-exposed United States occupations is about 19% below that of less-exposed peers, mainly through less hiring, a pattern they call descriptive, not causal [S26, S_CANARIES]. In Australia the evidence is mixed. The Department of Employment and Workplace Relations (DEWR) finds that the most-exposed fifth of Australian occupations grew 5.6% from November 2022 to February 2026, against 9.5% for the least exposed, a gap that may partly reflect earlier trends [S2]. It also finds employment of 20 to 24 year olds growing slightly faster than for older workers, and the youth unemployment gap below its pre-COVID range [S2, S27]. SEEK data link AI with more early-career demand, not less [S31]. In September 2026, youth unemployment was 11.1% and overall unemployment 4.7% [S28].

This is why the paper does not ask Parliament to pay a universal income now. It asks Parliament to fix the adequacy of today's payments, which is a problem now, and to legislate the tests that would show whether AI has begun to displace work at scale.

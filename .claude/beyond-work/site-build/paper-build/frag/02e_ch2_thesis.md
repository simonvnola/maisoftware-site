### A good life, not jobs

The paper's thesis is that government's aim should be a good life for its citizens, not jobs for their own sake. A good life here means an income that keeps people out of poverty, security against shocks they cannot insure themselves, and the time and health to care, learn and take part in their communities. Paid work is one route to those things, and for most adults the main one: it brings income, skills, purpose and company. But it is a route, not the goal. The paper therefore measures success by poverty, security and wellbeing, and monitors employment rather than targeting it.

Three reasons support the thesis.

1. **Targeting jobs for their own sake treats people as resources.** When the number of jobs is the goal, any job counts, and the pressure falls on the people with least choice to take work on whatever terms are offered, including dangerous, demeaning or pointless work. At worst, where people must take whatever work is offered or lose the income they live on, it comes close to unfree labour. A floor that people can live on is what makes work a choice.
2. **History shows the cost of leaving the gains to the market.** In the industrial revolution output rose for decades before most living standards did, and people without a floor bore the costs (above). If AI raises output while reducing the demand for some kinds of work, a policy that measures success by jobs would push people into whatever work remains rather than share the gains.
3. **The evidence suggests the trade-off is smaller than feared.** In the model the recommended Stage 1 cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to 10.5% (Table 5.7). Trials of unconditional cash found modest effects on work: participation fell 3.9 points in the OpenResearch trial [S82], employment rose slightly in the second year of Finland's trial [S75], and recipients spent more time on study, care and creative work (Section 4; Table 3.1). The paper does not claim the effects on work are nil. It costs them (Section 6) and keeps a binding cap on them for any universal payment (Table 9.6). The cap is not a jobs target: it protects the tax base that would pay for a universal payment, since a larger fall in paid work raises the net cost and the rate rise needed (Table 6.44).

This shapes the objectives. Objective 5 is a guardrail, not a target: work should stay a choice that pays. The employment-to-population ratio is reported against Treasury's projection as a risk indicator, with no target; the trial's work-effort cap stays binding; and the share of earning parents facing effective marginal tax rates above 50% is held down (Table 2.2). Objective 7 adds a measure of wellbeing.

Stage 1 does not fully meet the thesis, and the paper says so. The floor keeps JobSeeker's activity test: recipients must still look for work or lose payment, and suspensions are common [S60]. The paper keeps the test because removing or easing it is a reform in its own right, whose effects on claims, cost and employment the paper has not modelled or costed; none of its figures assume a change. This is the main place where the recommended Stage 1 falls short of the thesis. Recommendation 16 asks for a costed review of mutual obligation before the Bill is introduced (Section 9).

### The price of choosing Part 1

The 22 October paper recommended Part 1, though its own figures favoured P\* and the targeted package. This revision follows the figures. Part 1's reason was that its child payment is the one a universal Stage 2 would pay every child, so a later stage would change only the adult payment. That saves at most A$0.09bn to A$0.37bn once, in expectation, because even after a qualifying displacement Stage 2 passes the trial's work-effort cap with probability 0.16 to 0.21 at the OpenResearch trial's latest 3.9-point estimate. (At the 4.2 points the 22 October paper used, the figures were 0.09 to 0.14 and A$0.05bn to A$0.25bn.) That saving is measured against the targeted package; against P\*, which keeps the same schedule for most families, it is close to nil. The paper's method leaves out one other value of Part 1: paying the supplement to middle-income families as a universal entitlement, with its reach and payment channel. That cannot be priced; its break-even weights are in Table 6.12. Nothing the model review found lifts Part 1's advantage above about A$0.4bn once, against P\*'s A$0.8bn to A$3.1bn a year.

Against that saving, at the same budget Part 1 leaves:

- 0.20 million more people in poverty than P\* (0.8 points; children 0.6 points) and A$1.5bn to A$1.8bn a year less net benefit on the complete accounting, with more poverty in every group on every robustness run (Table 5.7);
- 0.56 million to 0.61 million more people in poverty than the targeted package, 2.2 to 2.4 points (Tables 5.12 and 5.13).

A targeted route reaches Part 1's poverty rate for much less. The floor, automatic enrolment, Rent Assistance +75% and the earned-income credit give 11.2% for A$40.4bn, A$22.4bn a year less than Part 1's budget (Table 5.11). No value the paper can name is worth that price, so Part 1 is rejected. Parliament could still choose it, but it would need to state the value it places on paying the middle-income supplement now and accept 0.20 million more people in poverty to get it.

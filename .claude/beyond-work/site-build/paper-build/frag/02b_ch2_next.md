### What is likely to happen next

Over the next ten years the outlook for work is uncertain, and the paper does not pretend otherwise. The evidence in the sections that follow supports four statements.

1. **Exposure is wide.** About 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed to AI, half of them likely to face lower demand [S23]; 34% of employment in high-income countries is in exposed occupations [S24]; and 4% of Australian workers are in occupations highly exposed to automation and 31% to augmentation [S25].
2. **There is no aggregate displacement yet.** Neither Australian nor overseas data show AI cutting employment across the economy [S29, S30, S_YALE26, S_DANISH].
3. **The early warning is in entry-level hiring.** In the United States, young workers in the most exposed occupations have fallen behind their peers, mainly through less hiring; in Australia the evidence is mixed [S2, S26, S_CANARIES].
4. **No one can yet put a probability on a large displacement.** Treasury expects AI to support productivity growth and finds no significant effects on the labour market yet, and most forecasts the Productivity Commission reviewed expect a net gain in jobs, though the Commission calls the job effects "highly uncertain" [S30, S32].

The paper therefore tests its proposal against a range rather than a forecast: a central path with no displacement, as in the Budget, and two scenarios of its own, in which AI displaces 0.67 million workers (moderate) or 1.77 million (severe) by 2035-36 (Table 6.54). The scenarios show what the system would have to cope with, not what is most likely.

The central benefit is the reduction in the household poverty gap after housing costs, valued with a distributional weight of 1.5 to 2.0, the author's value judgement. Table 6.12 applies that method to the three Stage 1 designs. On the complete accounting all three are negative: P\* −A$23.3bn to −A$2.3bn, the targeted package −A$25.2bn to nil and Part 1 −A$24.8bn to −A$4.1bn in 2028-29. Against Part 1, P\* gains A$1.5bn to A$1.8bn a year and the package −A$0.4bn to A$4.1bn, so Part 1 ranks below P\* on every reading and below the package on all but its least favourable one.

The method is narrow. It counts the poverty gap closed and the cost of the taxes, and nothing else: not health, care or the value of insurance. A broader method, the UK Green Book's, puts a weight on every household's gains and taxes according to its income (an inequality-aversion elasticity of 1.3). On that method all three designs turn positive: Part 1 at A$37.0bn to A$45.8bn, P\* at A$40.2bn to A$49.0bn and the package at A$50.9bn to A$59.7bn, so the same weights widen the package's lead (provisional). Table 6.12 also gives each design's break-even weight: the weight at which its net benefit reaches zero.

So the paper does not claim a positive monetised benefit for Stage 1. It claims that Stage 1 cuts poverty substantially at a cost the five taxes largely meet, and that among the designs of equal cost P\* is the paper's choice, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability. On every method the package is ahead of P\* or close to it: by A$2.0bn to A$4.3bn a year on the paper's method, by −A$1.9bn to A$2.3bn on the complete accounting and by about A$10.7bn with the Green Book's weights (Section 5 sets out the value judgement and its price).

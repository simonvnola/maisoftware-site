### Funding beyond the five taxes: an illustration

This section is illustrative. It asks how much safer Stage 1's funding would be with revenue from bases that depend neither on paid work nor on fossil fuels: capital gains, rental losses, land and inheritances. None of the measures here has a PBO costing, which every measure in the funding stack has, and none is recommended. The figures are first-round arithmetic on published Treasury, ABS and Productivity Commission figures, run on P\*'s funding rule (Table 6.6). They count no response of the five taxes, of P\*'s cost or of households to the new measures. The household model has not been run on them, so the paper gives no poverty or distributional result for them.

The reason to look is the funding risk already stated. The five taxes must yield 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45, and 101.7% for none to 2045-46. Three of the five rest on coal, minerals, gas and fuel, and the gas measure falls from A$19.7bn in 2031-32 to A$4.3bn in 2045-46 (Table 6.4).

Table 6.60 prices four measures.

- **Halving the capital gains discount.** Treasury puts the revenue forgone through the 50% discount for individuals and trusts at A$20.4bn in 2028-29, an estimate it rates as of medium to low reliability [S296]. Revenue forgone is not the revenue a change would raise, because people would hold assets longer. The paper takes half of it for a discount of 25%, and 75% of that (50% to 100%) for the response: A$7.6bn in 2028-29 (A$5.1bn to A$10.2bn).
- **Quarantining rental losses.** In 2022-23 rental losses of A$11.0bn gave their owners a tax benefit of about A$3.9bn [S296]. If losses could be set only against later rental income and gains, part of that benefit would be deferred, not removed. The paper scales the benefit by Treasury's projection of rental deductions and counts half of it (25% to 75%): A$3.2bn in 2028-29 (A$1.6bn to A$4.8bn).
- **A levy on land.** The ABS values all land in Australia at A$11,396.7bn at 30 June 2026 [S297]. A flat Commonwealth levy of 0.1% of unimproved value (0.05% to 0.2%), with 90% collected, would raise A$11.7bn in 2028-29 (A$5.8bn to A$23.3bn). For scale, the states raised A$20.0bn in land tax in 2024-25 on a much narrower base [S260]. The base here includes government land and every home, and no fall in land prices is counted.
- **A duty on large estates and gifts.** The Productivity Commission found that about 90% of wealth transfers are inheritances and that transfers could rise fourfold in real terms by 2050 [S298]. Its 2018 total, about A$120bn, is taken here from secondary coverage and needs checking. A duty that collected 4% of the inherited flow (2% to 6%) from 1 July 2029 would raise A$9.4bn in 2029-30 (A$4.7bn to A$14.2bn). This is the weakest of the four estimates.

Three results follow.

1. **The two investor tax changes alone remove the levy.** With them the 2045-46 shortfall of A$16.6bn becomes a surplus of A$8.2bn in the central case and a shortfall of A$1.3bn in the low case, which the surpluses kept from earlier years cover. P\* still borrows in its first year, A$9.9bn (A$5.7bn to A$14.0bn), unless the start is phased.
2. **All four leave a wide margin, on these assumptions.** The revenue could fall to 66.0% of its costing (54.5% to 78.2%) before a levy was needed, against 101.7% now.
3. **The funding rule would cover a top-up for children.** With all four measures the top-up of Table 6.58, which ends poverty for children under 15, is funded with no levy in every case, though in the low case the first year's borrowing is A$15.5bn.

In options C to G the rule accumulates large surpluses, because the added measures raise more than P\* needs. The paper does not show those balances as a target. They measure the room the measures would leave for other uses, which the paper does not cost.

#### A resource fund

The minerals and gas measures rest on resources that run down. A fund could turn them into lasting income, as the paper's dividend options could not (Section 10). Table 6.62 pays both measures into a fund in full and lets Stage 1 draw 4% of the opening balance each year, at the paper's central return of 6.5% [S132].

The fund holds A$551.7bn at 30 June 2046 (A$336.7bn in 2026 dollars), and by 2045-46 its draw, A$20.7bn, matches the A$20.9bn the two measures raise that year. Stage 1 could give up the two measures only with the four added ones, and then only in the central and high cases without a levy. With them it borrows A$8.3bn in 2028-29 and needs no levy to 2045-46 in the central and high cases; in the low case it borrows A$18.3bn and levies A$12.0bn in 2032-33. Without them it would need levies of A$323.5bn from 2032-33. The early borrowing costs the debt rate, 4.13%, so the fund gains only if it earns more than that.

#### What the illustration does not show

The four measures do little for the universal pathway. In 2032-33 they raise A$38.9bn (A$21.0bn to A$64.0bn), which is 1.9 points (1.0 to 3.1) of the 12.0 points Stage 2 needs on every income-tax rate on the static measure (Table 6.40), and only if none of it were used for Stage 1.

The illustration also leaves out what a costing would add: each measure's effect on the others and on the five taxes, the response of asset prices and rents, who would pay, and what the measures would do to poverty. A land levy on every home and a duty on estates would reach households that the five taxes do not. Recommendation 19 asks the PBO to cost the four measures and the fund, and Treasury to model who pays, before the committee considers them.

### Second-round effects of a displacement: claims, debt and asset prices

The displacement scenarios above count what a shock costs the Budget and how the levy meets it. They leave out three second-round effects that would decide what a displacement feels like to the people in it. A surge of claims would land on a means-tested system built for a steady caseload. Workers moved from a wage to the floor would keep the debts they took on at the wage. And some would sell assets to live, which would push prices down where they sold. This section sets out each effect, what the model can say about it, what it cannot, and what evidence would settle it. The model's figures are provisional: the runs were made for this revision and rest on assumptions stated in the notes. The external evidence was checked for the author on 1 November 2026 (Section 11).

The scenarios are those of Tables 6.50 and 6.51: a moderate shock (the employment-to-population ratio 3 points lower and wages 3% lower, phased in over 2032-33 to 2035-36) and a severe one (8 points and 8%). Recipients of replaced payments rise 15% or 40%.

#### A surge of claims on a means-tested system

By 2035-36 the moderate scenario displaces 0.67 million workers and adds 0.95 million recipients, 93% of today's JobSeeker-group stock; the severe scenario displaces 1.77 million and adds 2.53 million, 248% of the stock (Table 6.54). The inflow over 2032-33 to 2035-36 is about 164,000 newly displaced workers a year (159,000 to 173,000) in the moderate scenario and 437,000 (425,000 to 462,000) in the severe, 0.7 to 0.8 and 1.9 to 2.1 times the baseline flow of retrenchments. Services Australia granted 462,000 to 510,000 JobSeeker claims a year over 2023-24 to 2025-26, and 77,600 to 80,000 Youth Allowance jobseeker claims, finalising 528,000 JobSeeker claims in 2025-26 and 606,000 in 2023-24, and taking on average 22 days to process one in 2023-24 against a standard of 80% within 16 days [S_SA_AR2324, S_SA_AR2425, S_SA_AR2526, S_DSS_PROC]. The moderate inflow is 0.32 to 0.35 times a normal year's JobSeeker grants and the severe inflow 0.86 to 0.95 times, added on top of them. The system has done this once: from 16 March to 8 May 2020 it finalised 1,073,850 JobSeeker claims, in 10 days on average, with claims on hand peaking at 443,507 on 9 April and 7,595 staff added in two months [S_SA_SENATE, S_ANAO_COVID]. Those are finalised claims, not lodgements, so they show the system's throughput under pressure, not the limit of it.

Under the status quo and under Stage 1 alike, a displaced worker has to claim, and the claim is assessed against an income test, a partner income test, an assets test and, for people with savings, a liquid-assets waiting period. The floor changes the rate of the payment, not the gate to it. The gates are set in dollars. A partnered claimant gets nothing once the partner earns A$2,711.67 a fortnight gross [S_SA_PARTNER]. The assets test cancels payment when assessable assets, the home excluded, exceed A$333,000 for a single homeowner (A$600,000 for a non-homeowner) or A$499,000 for a couple (A$766,000) [S_SA_ASSETS]. The waiting period starts at A$5,500 of liquid assets for a single person and A$11,000 for a couple or a parent, and reaches its 13-week maximum at A$11,500 and A$23,000; liquid assets include a mortgage offset account but not a redraw balance, and hardship waivers apply [S_LAWP, S_DSS_GUIDE]. Most displaced workers have a partner: 61.8% of displaced households are couples and 58.7% have a partner with income (moderate scenario), and the median household income after displacement is A${{MED_INC_SQ}} under the status quo and A${{MED_INC_FLOOR}} on the floor. The partner income test removes 31.2% of displaced households from any payment on the floor, whose cut-out is A$87,728 a year in 2028-29, and 36.8% under the status quo, whose cut-out is A$74,531 a year in 2028-29 dollars (A$2,711.67 a fortnight today); among couples, half get nothing on the floor and 60% under the status quo (severe scenario: 30.7% and 36.5% of all displaced households). How many the waiting period would delay, or the assets test would exclude, cannot be estimated: the model carries tenure and mortgage debt but no other assets, the paper's worked households assume no assets test (Table 6.58, C36), and the published ABS wealth tables do not jointly identify employed adults, their partners and assessable assets against each threshold [S_ABS_HIW]. A tabulation of Survey of Income and Housing or HILDA microdata is needed [S223].

The pathway changes this only if a later Act legislates a universal payment, which reaches every adult without a claim. Before any such Act the pathway changes nothing. That is the strongest practical argument for building the payment rail now: the system that could pay every adult from ATO records is what a surge needs even if no universal rate is ever legislated. Section 7 proposes a surge protocol that uses it within Stage 1, and Table 6.54 gives what the model can price of it: enrolling the displaced automatically from payroll data costs A${{ENROL_COST_MODERATE}}bn (moderate) or A${{ENROL_COST_SEVERE}}bn (severe) a year by 2035-36 at the floor, the cost of reaching people who would otherwise not claim; waiving the waiting period costs at most A${{WAIVER_COST_MODERATE}}bn to A${{WAIVER_COST_SEVERE}}bn a year during the inflow, less in proportion to the share actually subject to it.

#### Debt that was taken on at a wage

A worker who moves from a wage to the floor keeps the mortgage and the other debts they took on at the wage. Of the 0.66 million households with a displaced worker in the moderate scenario, 0.35 million have a mortgage and 0.22 million rent privately. Their median repayment is A${{DEBT_MEDIAN_REPAYMENT_MODERATE}} a year, more than the single floor of A$41,438 in 2035-36; what keeps most of them going is a partner's income. On the floor {{DEBT_ABOVE30_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 of those mortgage holders pay more than 30% of their remaining household income in repayments and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 more than half, against {{DEBT_ABOVE30_SQ_MODERATE}},000 and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_SQ_MODERATE}},000 under the status quo (Table 6.55). In the severe scenario the counts are {{DEBT_ABOVE30_FLOOR_SEVERE}},000 and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_SEVERE}},000 on the floor, against {{DEBT_ABOVE30_SQ_SEVERE}},000 and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_SQ_SEVERE}},000. Of the {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 on the floor in the moderate scenario, about 25,000 would be likely to exhaust their buffers within three months, on the 2022 finding that about 40% of all loans held under three months of repayments, and 38,000 would have longer; in the severe scenario 67,000 and 101,000 [S_RBA_FSR2022]. Among private renters, {{RENT_ABOVE30_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 pay more than 30% of income in rent on the floor and {{RENT_ABOVE30_PSTAR_MODERATE}},000 with P\*'s higher Rent Assistance, against {{RENT_ABOVE30_SQ_MODERATE}},000 under the status quo; above 50%, P\*'s Rent Assistance rise cuts displaced renters from {{RENT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 to {{RENT_ABOVE50_PSTAR_MODERATE}},000.

So the floor cuts the status quo's count of mortgage holders who cannot service their loan by about a quarter, and no more. No safety-net rate services a mortgage set at a full-time wage. The pathway changes nothing before a later Act, and a universal payment at the JobSeeker rate with the floor as a top-up would pay a displaced single adult little more than the floor does.

How long households could hold on depends on buffers, lenders and partners, and the model holds none of them. The evidence sets the scale. The Reserve Bank's October 2026 review puts the median mortgage borrower's offset and redraw balances at over a year of scheduled repayments [S_RBA_FSR]; in 2022 just under 40% of all loans had less than three months of repayments in buffers, over half of them fixed-rate or investor loans [S_RBA_FSR2022, S_RBA_FSR2023]; and in 2024 around half of variable-rate owner-occupiers could fund six months of repayments plus essential spending from buffers if all income stopped [S_RBA_FSR2024]. Non-performing housing loans were 1.01% of residential credit in June 2026, against a series peak of 1.11% in June 2020 [S_APRA]. Lenders' hardship arrangements usually run up to three months [S_ASIC782]; after serious arrears, borrowers in the 2015 to 2019 securitised-loan sample took a median of three months to repay or refinance and six to cure or be foreclosed, and loans deep in negative equity had about a 50% chance of foreclosure [S_BERGMANN]. None of this is an unemployment-to-sale clock, and the model's A$10,000 buffer is an illustration, not a measured distribution; the shares of owner-occupiers below one, three and six months of buffers, and the time from job loss to sale, remain unsourced. Section 9 proposes a hardship protocol with APRA and lenders as an option the paper cannot price.

#### Forced sales and asset prices

Households that cannot service their debt sell. The paper cannot say how many would, or how far prices would fall, and it no longer offers a range. What it offers are two stress tests and the evidence on what forced sales do to prices.

The stress tests take the displaced mortgage holders paying more than half their remaining income in repayments and assume they all sold within a year: {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 households in the moderate scenario on the floor and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_SEVERE}},000 in the severe, against {{DEBT_ABOVE50_SQ_MODERATE}},000 and {{DEBT_ABOVE50_SQ_SEVERE}},000 under the status quo. Against the 560,005 residential dwelling transfers the ABS recorded in 2025-26 (provisional; 545,540 a year on average over the five years to 2025-26), they would add {{SALES_SHARE_FLOOR_MODERATE}} and {{SALES_SHARE_FLOOR_SEVERE}} to a year's sales (Table 6.56) [S_ABS_TVD]. A repayment-to-income threshold does not establish a sale, and the counts are scenario inputs, not a measured sell-through rate. The model has no investor households, so sales of investment properties are not counted; 2.02 million households, 21%, owned another residential property in 2019-20, holiday homes included [S_ABS_HOC], and a displacement would add their sales.

The price effect is not quantified. The evidence review found no defensible conversion from a count of forced sales to a fall in Australia's national prices, and the Reserve Bank's housing model cannot supply one, because its price response is to new dwellings added to the stock, which a resale does not add [S_SAUNDERS_TULIP]. What the evidence shows is local. In Massachusetts a foreclosed home sold at a 27% discount and lowered the price of homes within about 80 metres by about 1% [S_CAMPBELL]; in four United States cities each foreclosure listing within about 160 metres lowered nearby sale prices by about 1.6% [S_ANENBERG, S_COHEN]; and in Sydney's Blacktown each extra concurrent listing within 250 metres lowered a sale price by 0.87% (95% interval 0.26% to 1.48%), with ordinary listings, not distressed ones, and no effect in several other Sydney regions [S_SIJP_LI]. One national benchmark exists and does not transfer: in the United States crisis, 10% more homes on the market went with about 4 points lower prices, and only about half of each extra foreclosure added to net listings, because other sellers withdrew [S_MIAN]. If it applied here, the floor's stress tests would mean prices about 4.5 points (moderate) and 12.0 points (severe) lower; it does not apply, because it comes from listings in a crisis, not Australian annual sales, and the paper reports it only as an imported figure, not an estimate. Local spillovers cannot be summed into a national result, because a national effect depends on where the sellers are, who buys, credit, and the fall in incomes that caused the sales; a spatial housing model is needed, and Recommendation 15c asks for one.

A fall in prices bears on four things. Stamp duty and land tax are state taxes outside the model: the states raised A$34.4bn in conveyance duty and A$20.0bn in land tax in 2024-25 [S_ABS_TAXREV], and New South Wales puts a one-point rise in transacted prices at A$122m of duty and a one-point rise in volumes at A$98m in 2026-27, so forced sales, which lower prices but raise volumes, pull its duty both ways [S_NSW_BP1]. The five taxes feel a price fall only through the excess profits tax, if bank profits fall with bad debts; the PBO's costing discloses no bank share, so the paper can only test the tax's yield [S146, S_PBO_EPT]. At 90% of its costing, P\*'s first levy moves from 2043-44 to 2032-33 and its levies to 2045-46 from A$36.5bn to A$124.2bn (Table 6.56). Banks' losses are not estimated; the Reserve Bank's stress tests cover them [S_RBA_FSR]. And rents: the paper's rent scenario (Table 6.31) raises rents by 6.6% to 32.4% of renters' gains, a demand effect of Stage 1's cash in normal times. In a displacement the direction of rents is not established. Owners who lose a mortgaged home become renters, which adds demand; investors who sell to owner-occupiers take a dwelling out of the rental stock but also a household out of rental demand [S_AHURI291]; displaced renters' incomes fall, which lowers what they can pay; and landlords pass little of their own costs into rents [S_RBA_RENTS]. No Australian study identifies the sign or size of rents in the two years after such a shock. So the 0.70 million recipient households worse off after housing costs under P\* in normal times (Table 6.21) could grow or shrink in a displacement; the paper does not know which, and says so.

The displacement, not the design, drives these effects. Stage 1 changes them only by replacing a lower payment with the floor, which cuts the households that cannot service their loan by about a quarter, and the pathway not at all before a later Act. What would reduce forced sales is not making people sell to pay, through a hardship protocol with lenders, and not making them sell an investment property to qualify, through a deferral of the assets test; neither can be priced, the first because its cost falls on lenders on terms the paper cannot set, the second because the model holds no asset data (Recommendation 15c).

#### What the model cannot estimate, and the evidence needed

The model cannot estimate how many displaced workers the waiting period and the assets test would catch, how long households could draw on savings, how lenders would respond, or how far prices would fall; the last three interact, and a price fall deepens the debt problem it started from. The paper's displacement scenarios also hold GDP at baseline, and the status-quo cost of displacement (A$71.4bn to A$185.1bn a year in 2035-36, Table 6.52) is the direct Budget cost, before any of these effects. The evidence review of 1 November 2026 sourced the thresholds, the claims throughput, the 2020 surge, the buffers, the arrears and the state taxes; what it could not find is shorter. Still missing are: the shares of employed adults above each means-test threshold, which need a tabulation of wealth microdata; the shares of owner-occupiers below one, three and six months of buffers, and the time from job loss to sale, which need the Reserve Bank's and lenders' loan-level data; claims lodged by month and the share granted automatically in 2020, which Services Australia holds; the bank share of the excess profits tax, which the PBO's costing does not disclose; a spatial housing model that could turn forced listings into a price path; and the response of rents to such a shock, which no Australian study identifies. Recommendation 15c asks for them before the Bill, and Table 9.5 adds two indicators so that a surge would be measured as it happened.

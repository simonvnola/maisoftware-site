### The cash guarantee, and who is worse off after housing costs

The 22 October paper said no current recipient would lose income. That holds only for payments. The guarantee this paper proposes is narrower and is stated as such: no one who receives, or would qualify for, a payment Stage 1 changes will be paid less than under today's rules. Under P\* the only payment replaced is Family Tax Benefit, so the guarantee works through family payment protection: every family keeps at least its current-rules FTB, indexed as current rules would index it. The Act defines the protected group by the payments' income and assets tests.

The guarantee does not cover income after housing costs. Pensioners' payments do not change at Stage 1, but in the model's rent and interest-rate scenario rents and mortgage rates rise as the reform lifts other incomes. On the paper's central scenario (45 basis points on variable mortgages and the middle of the rent pass-through range), 0.70 million recipient households (1.65 million people, 16% of recipient households) are worse off after housing costs under P\*, by A$770 a year on average (Table 6.21, provisional). Under Part 1 the figure was 1.11 million households (2.30 million people, 25% of recipient households), losing A$933 a year on average, including 60% of Disability Support Pension households and 84% of privately renting Age Pension households. No JobSeeker, Youth Allowance or Parenting Payment household is worse off under either design.

P\*'s Rent Assistance rise removes 37% of Part 1's losers, not most of them. Privately renting Age Pension households fall from 84% to 25% worse off and Disability Support Pension households from 60% to 34%, but pensioners who own their home or have a mortgage do not move: about 15% are worse off under both designs. The rent safeguard raises Rent Assistance further if rents outpace wages (Table 9.6), but it does not reach owners.

If a later Act legislated a universal payment, the guarantee would need more parts: a Recipient Protection Offset, so that protected recipients pay no more tax on other income than under current rules, capped at the tax current rules levy on the payment; and a phase-out of protection at 50 cents a dollar above a phase-out point, so there are no cliffs. Those parts would create a protected class worth A$21,000 to A$50,000 a year to the worked households at Stage 3 (Table 6.28), which people may claim a payment to acquire [S134]. A net-income guarantee avoids that and costs less, but protected recipients would gain little at later stages (Table 6.30). The choice belongs to the Act that would set a rate.

### The universal pathway: tests, not rates

The universal stages are a pathway Parliament can take if AI displaces work at scale. The Act would legislate three tests now and no rates (Table 5.15).

1. **An AI need test**, over the same 8 consecutive quarters. Youth unemployment must be at least 3 percentage points above its September 2026 level of 11.1%. Employment of 22 to 29 year olds in the most AI-exposed fifth of occupations must fall at least 5% relative to the least exposed, on JSA's series. Measured as a change since November 2022, the AI leg cannot stay met indefinitely, so a later general recession does not meet it. Productivity growth cannot start a stage. On the Budget's forecasts the test is not met; meeting it needs the employment-to-population ratio to fall at least 2 points.
2. **A randomised trial with a work-effort cap.** About 5,000 working-age adults not on a payment receive a payment at the JobSeeker rate from July 2029 to June 2031, against a control group. If participation falls more than 3 points, with the lower end of its 90% confidence interval above 1 point, no universal payment may be proposed under the Act.
3. **A PBO certificate.** The PBO costs any proposed rate afresh and certifies that funding at least equals net cost in each of the next four years; for a payment at the Age Pension rate, also under a doubled taxpayer response and the combined downside of Section 6. Under the AI receipts rule half of tax receipts above the 2026-27 Budget's projections, fixed when the Act passes and indexed to prices, count toward that funding; non-AI windfalls count too.

Only when all three are met may a Minister introduce a Bill setting a rate, and Parliament then votes on it. In a displacement declared after the need test's youth unemployment leg has been met for eight quarters, the floor and child payment keep their indexation and a levy meets any shortfall (Table 6.51).

What the pathway would cost comes from runs of the 22 October design (Part 1 with the wealth-tax fund's dividend), which have not been repeated on P\*; they show the order of magnitude, not P\*'s exact figures. A payment to every adult at the JobSeeker rate (Stage 2) would need every income-tax rate about 15.6 points higher on the smallest displacement that meets the need test, and 18.6 to 25.5 points on slower paths timed to the trigger (Table 6.38). The trial's own evidence makes it unlikely to start. At the 4.2-point fall in participation the 22 October paper used, the work-effort cap would bind with probability 0.86 to 0.91; at the trial's latest estimate of 3.9 points, with probability 0.79 to 0.84. A payment at the Age Pension rate (Stage 3) would need about 30.9 points, and no rate passes its certificate on any modelled path: a levy on market income alone cannot fund it, and under a doubled taxpayer response or the combined downside no rate rise does (Table 6.48). The paper therefore describes Stage 3 and does not authorise it.

Why legislate tests that will probably never be met? Because the tests, the data series and the trial take years to build, and the time to agree them is before a crisis, not during one. If AI cuts demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments, so targeting stops saving money and steep tapers fall on nearly everyone; a universal payment would then be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests are cheap: the trial costs about A$120m a year for two years, and the indicators and certificates about A$10m a year (Table 6.2). The pathway may never be used, and the paper says so (Section 8).

Legislating no rate also removes a risk the 22 October paper conceded: a contingent rise of 16 to 31 points written into law could change where high earners settle and when they realise income, before any trigger.

### A dividend: left for later

The 22 October paper set out four ways to pay a yearly dividend to every adult and left the choice to Parliament. None reduces poverty: Stage 1 gives the same poverty rate with or without a dividend, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the line with it. This revision recommends none of them now and moves them to their own chapter, Section 10, with the tests any dividend should pass before Parliament legislates it.

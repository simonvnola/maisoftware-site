## 10. A dividend: options for later

A dividend is a yearly payment to every adult, the most visible universal element a basic income could have. The 22 October paper set out four ways to pay one and left the choice to Parliament. This revision recommends none of them now, for three reasons drawn from the paper's own figures. No dividend reduces poverty: Stage 1 gives the same poverty rate with or without one, because a payment to everyone lifts the median and the poverty line with it (Table 10.4). After the taxes that pay for it, about a third of households are better off under every option, the same share as with no dividend (Table 10.2). And every option scores below Stage 1 alone on the paper's net-benefit method (Table 10.2).

This chapter sets the options out neutrally, with the tests any of them should pass before Parliament legislates it. Every table in it was run on the 22 October design, Part 1, with the option shown; none has been rerun on P\*, and on P\* the figures, especially the levy dates, would differ.

### What a fund could and could not do

If Parliament later wants a fund, its case is narrower than the 22 October paper made it. A fund is public wealth: the wealth-tax fund would hold A$325.3bn at 30 June 2046 (A$203.5bn in 2026 dollars), and in a declared displacement it could lend the Budget up to a quarter of its balance, A$56.6bn in 2035 or A$75.6bn in 2040 (Table 10.2). That loan bridges about a year (Table 6.51). A fund is not much of a source of income. The wealth-tax fund's dividend is A$47 in 2029-30 and A$461 in 2045-46, and on the high reading of the wealth tax's losses in other taxes the fund runs out in 2083-84 (Table 10.24). Its return is a risk the recipients bear: at the bond rate it pays A$383 in 2045-46 and is exhausted by 2067-68 (Table 10.12). A surplus fund lasts at the central return but pays a token dividend, A$1 in 2032-33 and A$86 in 2045-46.

Universal cash tied to volatile revenue has been cut or eroded where it has been tried. Alaska's Governor halved the 2016 dividend by veto, and the state Supreme Court upheld the cut [S99, S195]; Alaska's 2025 dividend was its smallest in real terms [S_ALASKA25]. Iran's near-universal transfer lost purchasing power to inflation and sanctions [S100].

### The tests any dividend should pass

Before Parliament legislates any dividend, the paper proposes five tests.

1. **A PBO costing** of the option against the 2026-27 Budget baseline, and for a fund a ruling on its Budget treatment (Recommendation 12c).
2. **A stated value on universality.** Parliament states what it values paying every adult at, per adult a year, and the option's break-even weight must be below it. On the 22 October run the weights are A$171 to A$657 for the wealth-tax fund, A$22 to A$65 for the Budget-paid dividend and A$20 to A$37 for the surplus fund (Table 10.2).
3. **Financing separate from Stage 1's.** The option's financing is stated separately from the five durable taxes, with the levy it adds and the year it brings Stage 1's first levy forward to. On the 22 October run the dividend options brought it from 2044-45 to between 2038-39 and 2040-41 (Table 10.2).
4. **Rules described as statutory.** Any never-cut guarantee and any rule protecting a fund's capital are statutory, and a later Parliament can change them; the Act would say so.
5. **Delivery reported.** Lodgers are paid at assessment and non-lodgers by Services Australia after enrolment, with take-up reported (80% is assumed, with no Australian precedent; Table 10.15); the dividend is excluded from every adjusted-taxable-income test (Table 10.16).

## Changes in this revision

This version supersedes all earlier versions, including the paper of 22 October 2026. An external critique of that paper made ten criticisms. The author accepts five in full and four in part; the remaining one, that winners were headlined before the funding taxes (criticism 5), holds for the online calculator rather than the paper. The main change is that the recommendation now follows from the paper's own comparison.

1. **Targeted welfare called basic income.** Accepted in part. The paper is retitled. Stage 1 is called what it is, a targeted floor, and the universal stages are called a conditional pathway (Sections 1 and 5).
2. **It recommended about 0.56 million more people in poverty.** Accepted. Stage 1 is now P\*, the design the paper's own figures favour over Part 1. The targeted package is offered as Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty above work incentives, and the price of preferring P\* to it is stated. Part 1 is shown as the rejected option, with its price (Section 5).
3. **Its own appraisal found Stage 1 destroys value.** Accepted in part. The summary now says every Stage 1 design's net benefit is negative on the complete accounting and spans zero on the paper's method, and ranks them, gives each design's break-even distributional weight, and says the case rests on cutting poverty, weighed against work incentives and fiscal sustainability, not on a positive monetised benefit (Sections 1 and 6).
4. **The universal destination is blocked or unaffordable.** Accepted. Parliament is asked to legislate the tests (an AI need test, a randomised trial and a PBO certificate), not rates. Stage 3 is described, not authorised (Section 5).
5. **Winners were headlined before the funding taxes.** Accepted for the calculator. The paper now leads with results after the funding taxes and gives each household decile's share of the five taxes (Sections 1 and 6).
6. **"No current recipient loses" is narrow.** Accepted. The guarantee is restated as what it is, that no one's payment falls under today's rules, and the households worse off after housing costs are counted (Sections 5 and 6).
7. **The Budget advance is borrowing.** Accepted. The summary calls it borrowing and gives the amount. A joint stress test gives the odds of an earlier levy, and a phased start that avoids the borrowing is costed (Sections 1 and 6).
8. **The default dividend adds machinery.** Accepted in part. The dividend options move to their own chapter with their own tests, and none is recommended. The central fiscal path is rebuilt on the recommended design with no dividend (Sections 6 and 10).
9. **Precision exceeds predictive power.** Accepted in part. Uncertain dates and rates are given as ranges, and each table names the design it was run on. The calculator's fixes are listed separately.
10. **The easy objections were answered best.** Accepted. "Why legislate any universal pathway?" is now the first objection, followed by "Why not the targeted package?" and "Who pays?" (Section 8).

Three corrections were made while answering the critique.

- **P\* as first costed was not at Part 1's budget.** The variant the 22 October paper reported (A$6,973 a child, with automatic enrolment) cost A$6.2bn more than Part 1 in 2028-29 on the fiscal model's basis. The recommended P\* is re-costed at Part 1's budget: A$6,750 a child, the floor and Rent Assistance +75%, without automatic enrolment. On that basis it leaves 10.5% of people in poverty after housing costs, not 10.1%; 0.20 million fewer people in poverty than Part 1, not 0.32 million; and A$1.5bn to A$1.8bn a year more net benefit than Part 1, not A$2.4bn to A$3.2bn (Table 5.7).
- **The OpenResearch trial's fall in labour-force participation is 3.9 percentage points**, the figure in the trial's latest revision [S82], not 4.2. At 3.9 points the trial's work-effort cap binds with probability 0.79 to 0.84, not 0.86 to 0.91, so a universal Stage 2 would pass with probability 0.16 to 0.21, not 0.09 to 0.14, and Part 1's expected saving as Stage 2's base rises from A$0.05bn to A$0.25bn to A$0.09bn to A$0.37bn once. The paper uses the 3.9-point figures throughout.
- **"No current recipient loses" did not hold after housing costs.** On Part 1, 1.11 million recipient households were worse off once higher rents and interest rates were counted (Table 6.21).

The paper also adds newer evidence on AI and jobs (Section 2), and a new section on three second-round effects of a displacement that earlier versions ignored: a surge of claims, debt service on the floor and forced sales (Section 6), checked against an external evidence review of 1 November 2026, with a surge protocol (Section 7), three new risks and an objection (Section 8) and recommendations and options with their prices where the model can give them (Section 9). The review found no defensible way to turn forced sales into a national price fall, so the paper gives none. Table 8.6 gives each criticism, the answer and where the paper changed.

The paper is also restructured around one argument: where Australia is now, what is likely to happen next, two solutions (a targeted floor now and a tested, pre-built pathway to a universal payment), and what in the design could serve other countries (Sections 1, 2, 5 and 9). It states its thesis, that government's aim should be a good life for citizens, not jobs for their own sake, adds the lesson of the industrial revolution, turns the employment objective into a guardrail that is monitored, not targeted, and says plainly where Stage 1 falls short of the thesis: it keeps the activity test (Sections 2, 5 and 9).

## 1. Executive summary

This paper argues that government's aim in the AI transition should be a good life for Australians, not jobs for their own sake. Paid work is one route to a good life, and for most people the main one, but it is a route, not the goal. A government that targets job growth for its own sake treats citizens as resources to be used and, at worst, presses them into whatever work is left. In Britain's industrial revolution the only floor was the Poor Law, and after 1834 the workhouse, which made relief deliberately harsh so that people would take any work instead; for decades many took dangerous and degrading work [S_HUMPHRIES, S_POORLAW]. The paper therefore measures success by poverty, security and wellbeing, and monitors employment rather than targeting it (Section 2). Its argument has four parts.

### Where Australia is now

The safety net already leaves millions in poverty. In 2022-23, 3.7 million people, 14.2%, lived below the poverty line after housing costs, including 757,000 children, 15.6% of children under 15 [S37]. In households relying on JobSeeker the rate was 76% [S39]: JobSeeker pays a single adult A$412.45 a week against a poverty line of A$569.81 [S3, S4, S40]. The system ties security to paid work through means tests and an activity test, which bring errors, suspensions and debts; Robodebt raised about A$1.76bn in alleged debts against about 526,000 people [S56]. Carers give about 2.2 billion hours of unpaid care a year, worth A$77.9bn, and the market pays nothing for it [S55]. The paper's targets are poverty after housing costs of 12.0% or lower for all persons and 7.0% or lower for children by 2030-31, and 55.5% or lower in JobSeeker-reliant households (Table 2.2).

### What is likely to happen next

AI capability is rising quickly, and many jobs are exposed to it. The International Monetary Fund (IMF) estimates that about 60% of jobs in advanced economies are exposed, half of them likely to face lower demand [S23]; the International Labour Organization puts 34% of employment in high-income countries in exposed occupations [S24]; and Jobs and Skills Australia finds 4% of Australian workers in occupations highly exposed to automation and 31% to augmentation [S25]. There is no aggregate displacement yet, but the first warning is in entry-level hiring in the United States, and the Australian evidence is mixed [S2, S26, S_CANARIES]. Over the next ten years the plausible range runs from no net loss of jobs, which most forecasts the Productivity Commission reviewed expect [S32], to 0.67 million (moderate) or 1.77 million (severe) workers displaced by 2035-36 in the paper's scenarios (Table 6.54). The paper cannot say which is more likely, and on current evidence no one can. The industrial revolution shows what is at stake if displacement comes: output rose for decades before most people's living standards did; relief under the Poor Law was made deliberately harsh, so many people, children among them, took dangerous and degrading work; and the protections came late and had to be fought for [S_ALLEN, S_FEINSTEIN, S_HUMPHRIES, S_FACTORY1833, S_POORLAW].

### Two solutions

1. **A targeted floor now (Stage 1, P\*).** From 1 July 2028, lift working-age payments to a floor at the poverty line, pay a child payment without cliffs in place of Family Tax Benefit, and raise Rent Assistance by 75%. In the model it cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to 10.5% and child poverty from 15.6% to 6.6% (Table 5.7). Among the designs of equal cost the paper compares, it is the paper's choice when poverty is weighed against work incentives and fiscal sustainability. The targeted package cuts poverty further and is Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty more heavily.
2. **A tested pathway to a universal payment, built before it is needed.** Legislate now the tests a universal payment would have to pass (an AI need test, a randomised trial with a work-effort cap and a Parliamentary Budget Office certificate) but no rate, and build the payment system that can reach every adult and a surge protocol that enrols displaced workers from payroll data. If displacement comes, Parliament can move quickly on evidence it has already agreed to accept; if it does not, the tests cost little: the trial, the largest part the paper can price, costs about A$120m a year for two years (Sections 5 and 7).

A dividend paid to every adult is an option for later, not a third solution: Section 10 sets out four options and the tests any of them should pass.

### A model beyond Australia

The design is Australian, but most of its logic is not. The second solution transfers most widely: any country with real-time payroll data, an independent fiscal institution that can certify costings and a broad revenue base can legislate its tests before a shock and build the means to pay quickly. The first transfers most directly to countries whose income support, like Australia's, is flat-rate, means-tested and paid from general revenue, such as New Zealand [S_NZJS], and needs adapting where contributory unemployment insurance dominates. The taxes that fund it are specific to Australia. Section 9 sets out what transfers and what does not, and recommends sharing the design through the OECD (Recommendation 17).

### What the paper recommends

Australia should fix the adequacy of income support now with a targeted floor. Among the designs of equal cost it compares, the paper chooses P\*, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability. It should also legislate the tests a universal basic income would have to pass, so the country can move quickly if AI displaces work at scale. It should not legislate a universal rate or a dividend yet, because on current evidence neither pays its way. The case for a basic income is insurance against a shock whose probability no one can yet estimate.

After the five taxes that pay for it are counted, 34.2% of adults live in households better off under the recommended Stage 1 in 2028-29, or 32.4% by A$5 a week or more, against 32.5% under Part 1; before the taxes, 65.3% do. Of households, 35.4% are better off after the taxes and 62.1% gain before them (Table 6.14, provisional). The taxes fall mostly on the top income decile, which bears 52.9% of them (Table 6.15). Poverty after housing costs falls from 14.2% to 10.5% in the model.

The recommended Stage 1, called P\* in this paper, starts on 1 July 2028 and has three parts:

- a floor for working-age payments at the poverty line, A$569.81 a week (A$32,570 a year in 2028-29);
- a child payment of A$6,750 a child, paid with each family's maximum Family Tax Benefit (FTB) and withdrawn at 18 cents a dollar of parents' income above A$72,631, with no cliffs; and
- a 75% rise in the maximum rates of Commonwealth Rent Assistance.

It is recommended over Part 1, the design the 22 October paper recommended, because the paper's own comparison favours it. At the same budget it leaves 0.20 million fewer people in poverty than Part 1, less poverty in every group shown and in every robustness run, and A$1.5bn to A$1.8bn a year more net benefit (A$0.8bn to A$3.1bn across the robustness runs). Part 1 paid a supplement to middle-income families so that a universal Stage 2 would later pay it to every child; that saves at most A$0.09bn to A$0.37bn in expectation, far less than its price (Table 5.7).

The targeted package is Parliament's alternative if it weights poverty above work incentives and fiscal risk. At the same cost it cuts poverty further, to 9.0% against 10.5%, and leaves 0.40 million fewer people in poverty than P\*. It is ahead of P\* by A$2.0bn to A$4.3bn a year on the paper's method and by about A$10.7bn a year with the Green Book's weights; on the complete accounting it is between A$1.9bn a year behind and A$2.3bn ahead. On average it also treats working families better: second earners' mean participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P\*. P\*'s advantages are at the extreme and in the funding. Under P\* 1.3% of higher earners in families with children face an effective tax rate of 60% or more, against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner does, against 6.8%. And the package needs A$108.1bn of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5bn, with an 87% against a 74% chance of a levy before 2044-45 (Tables 5.7, 6.9 and 6.12; provisional). Preferring P\* is a value judgement that those advantages are worth the price. Parliament may judge otherwise.

Five facts weigh against the recommendation, and Parliament should know them.

1. **The Budget advance is borrowing.** The five taxes fall A$20.7bn short of P\*'s cost in 2028-29 (provisional), and the Budget borrows the gap, repaid with interest by 30 June 2032 (Table 6.6). Starting the child payment a year after the floor would avoid the borrowing, at the price of a year without the child payment, which would have paid families A$26.4bn above the Family Tax Benefit they keep (the budget saves A$37.3bn) (Table 6.7).
2. **Every Stage 1 design has a negative net benefit on the complete accounting.** On the paper's method, which counts the economic cost of the taxes, the ranges span zero: P\* −A$12.1bn to A$3.3bn, the package −A$10.1bn to A$7.6bn and Part 1 −A$12.8bn to A$1.9bn a year in 2028-29. On the complete accounting, which also counts Stage 1's own behavioural costs, P\* scores −A$23.3bn to −A$2.3bn in 2028-29 and the targeted package −A$25.2bn to nil (both provisional), and Part 1 −A$24.8bn to −A$4.1bn. Part 1 ranks below P\* on every reading. The case for Stage 1 rests on cutting poverty, weighed against work incentives and fiscal sustainability, not on a positive monetised benefit. P\* breaks even at a distributional weight of 1.5 to 3.3 on the paper's method, or 2.3 to 4.9 on the complete accounting; the package at 1.3 to 2.4 or 2.0 to 3.8; and Part 1 at 1.6 to 3.9 or 2.8 to 6.1; against the 1.5 to 2.0 the paper uses (Table 6.12, provisional).
3. **The guarantee covers payments, not income after housing costs.** No one's payment falls under today's rules. But after higher rents and interest rates, 0.70 million recipient households (1.65 million people, 16% of recipient households) are worse off under P\*, by A$770 a year on average, mostly Disability Support Pension households (0.28 million) and Age Pension households that own or are buying their home (0.26 million), whom Rent Assistance does not reach. P\*'s Rent Assistance rise removes 37% of Part 1's 1.11 million (Table 6.21, provisional).
4. **The five taxes fall short each year from 2037-38.** Surpluses kept from the 2030s fill the gap until a levy on market income from 2043-44 or 2044-45 on central yields, depending on how Rent Assistance grows, totalling A$19.8bn to A$36.5bn to 2045-46 (provisional). A joint stress test puts the chance of a levy before 2044-45 at 48% to 74%, and before 2034-35 at 37% to 59% (Table 6.9, provisional). P\* carries the same funding risk as Part 1.
5. **The universal stages will probably never start.** Stage 2 would need every income-tax rate 15.6 to 25.5 points higher (15.6 on the smallest displacement that meets the need test, up to 25.5 on slower paths timed to the trigger), and at the trial's latest estimate the work-effort cap stops it with probability 0.79 to 0.84. A full basic income at the Age Pension rate (Stage 3) would need about 30.9 points, and no rate passes its funding test on any modelled path (Section 6).

A displacement would also have second-round effects the 22 October paper ignored. By 2035-36 the moderate scenario displaces 0.67 million workers and adds 0.95 million recipients, 93% of today's JobSeeker-group stock, and the severe scenario 1.77 million and 2.53 million; the severe inflow, about 437,000 workers a year, is 0.86 to 0.95 times the JobSeeker claims the system grants in a normal year, added on top of them, and in 2020 it finalised 1.07 million claims in eight weeks. Most displaced workers have a partner (62% of displaced households are couples), and the partner income test removes 31.2% of displaced households from any payment on the floor and 36.8% under the status quo. Displaced mortgage holders' median repayment, A${{DEBT_MEDIAN_REPAYMENT_MODERATE}} a year, exceeds the floor: on the floor {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_MODERATE}},000 (moderate) to {{DEBT_ABOVE50_FLOOR_SEVERE}},000 (severe) would pay more than half their remaining household income in repayments, about a quarter fewer than on JobSeeker. If they all sold within a year they would add {{SALES_SHARE_FLOOR_MODERATE}} to {{SALES_SHARE_FLOOR_SEVERE}} to a year's dwelling sales; the effect on prices is local in the evidence and not quantified nationally, and the direction of rents is not established (Tables 6.54 to 6.56; model figures provisional). The paper proposes a surge protocol for a declared displacement that enrols displaced workers from payroll data (A${{ENROL_COST_MODERATE}}bn to A${{ENROL_COST_SEVERE}}bn a year by 2035-36) and waives the liquid-assets waiting period (at most A${{WAIVER_COST_MODERATE}}bn to A${{WAIVER_COST_SEVERE}}bn a year), and offers an assets-test deferral and a hardship protocol with lenders as options it cannot price (Section 7; Recommendation 15). It does not claim that an income floor is insurance against a mortgage.

The universal stages therefore become a tested, pre-committed pathway. The Act would set the AI need test, the randomised trial and the Parliamentary Budget Office (PBO) certificate a universal payment must pass, and no rates. Stage 3 is described but not authorised. No dividend is recommended now; four options are set out in Section 10, each with its tests.

### Key risks

1. Revenue shortfall: the five taxes must yield 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45 under P\* (99.7% under Part 1); a joint stress test gives a 48% to 74% chance of a levy before then (Table 6.9).
2. Housing: after housing costs, 0.70 million recipient households are worse off under P\*, and on Part 1's run higher rents and interest rates lifted poverty by 0.3 to 0.7 points (Tables 6.21 and 6.32).
3. Early AI displacement: with no fund to lend, the levy in a severe shock reached 1.96% of market income in 2034-35 and 6.17% by 2045-46 on Part 1's run (Table 6.51).

### Decisions needed now (Table 9.1)

1. Pass the Australian Basic Income (ABI) Act with P\* as Stage 1 by 31 December 2027, or the targeted package if Parliament weights poverty above work incentives.
2. Legislate the five durable taxes, the AI need test, the randomised trial and the PBO certificate; legislate no universal rate and no dividend.
3. Refer this paper to a joint select committee reporting by 30 September 2027, and ask the PBO to cost P\* (Recommendation 12a).

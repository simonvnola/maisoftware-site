### The strongest objections, answered

The objections are ordered by weight: the three that decide whether the recommendation stands come first.

**Objection 1. Why legislate any universal pathway?** If a universal basic income will probably never pass its own tests, why put any of it in law? There is no aggregate displacement yet [S29, S_YALE26, S_DANISH]. On the trial's latest estimate the work-effort cap would stop a universal payment with probability {{WORK_CAP_ODDS_AT_3_9}}. A payment at the Age Pension rate would cost 12.2% of GDP and cannot be funded from income tax on the paper's central assumptions (Tables 6.35 and 6.48), and every costing of a full basic income the paper has found agrees on its scale [S57, S116, S_IMF17, S_OECD17].

The objection is right that the pathway will probably not be used, and the paper does not pretend otherwise. The case for legislating the tests is insurance against a shock whose probability no one can yet estimate. If AI cut demand for labour across the economy, most people would qualify for means-tested payments, so targeting would stop saving money and steep tapers would fall on nearly everyone; a universal payment would then be the simplest way to share the gains. The tests take years to build: JSA's need-test series, a two-year trial and a PBO method for certifying a rate. It is far easier to agree them before a crisis than during one. The premium is small, about A$120m a year for the two trial years and about A$10m a year for the indicators and certificates (Table 6.2), and the pathway pays nobody and sets no rate, so there is no contingent tax rise for high earners to anticipate. If the tests are never met, the money buys evidence the country would want anyway. The pathway may never be used, and that is an acceptable outcome for insurance.

Cheaper forms of insurance target AI job loss directly: wage insurance [S_NYFED], and unemployment insurance that grows more generous automatically when unemployment rises. They complement the pathway, and the committee could add either to Stage 1.

**Objection 2. Why not the targeted package?** It cuts poverty most: 9.0% against 10.5% for P\*, with 0.40 million fewer people in poverty, and less poverty in every group on every robustness run (Table 5.7). The World Bank found existing programs about 60% more effective than a budget-neutral basic income [S105], and the OECD found one would raise poverty [S114, S_OECD17].

On poverty the objection holds, and on most other measures too. Preferring P\* to the package is a value judgement, and the paper states its price on every method it uses. The package leaves about 0.40 million fewer people in poverty (1.5 points). On the paper's method it is A$2.0bn to A$4.3bn a year ahead of P\*; on the complete accounting it is between A$1.9bn a year behind and A$2.3bn ahead; with the Green Book's weights it is about A$10.7bn a year ahead. It breaks even at lower distributional weights: 1.3 to 2.4 on the paper's method and 2.0 to 3.8 on the complete accounting, against P\*'s 1.5 to 3.3 and 2.3 to 4.9 (Table 6.12, provisional). On average the package also treats working families better: second earners' mean participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P\*, 22.4% of second earners face 40% or more against 31.6%, and 21.8% of higher earners in families with children face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more against 41.6% (Table 5.7). P\*'s work-incentive advantage is only at the extreme: 1.3% of higher earners face 60% or more under P\* against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner faces 60% or more against 6.8%. The paper's second reason is the fiscal path. The package's costs grow faster, so it needs A$108.1bn of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5bn for P\*, and a levy before 2044-45 is more likely, 87% against 74% in the joint stress test (Tables 6.6 and 6.9; model results Table B5; provisional). The paper judges that avoiding those very high rates and the larger levy is worth the price. Parliament may reasonably judge otherwise, and should choose the package if it weights poverty above them. Both fit the same Act, and Parliament can legislate either as Stage 1 or add the package's earned-income credit to P\* later.

**Objection 3. Who pays?** Stage 1 is paid for by five taxes, by borrowing in its first year and, from the late 2030s, by a levy on market income. Its gains are headlined, its costs are not.

The objection is fair, and the paper now leads with the result after the taxes: {{PSTAR_HH_BETTER_AFTER_FUNDING_2028_29}} of households are better off under P\* and {{PSTAR_HH_WORSE_AFTER_FUNDING_2028_29}} worse off on the central allocation, or {{PSTAR_HH_WORSE_INCIDENCE_RANGE}} across the incidence variants (Table 6.14). The five taxes fall mostly on the top income decile, but the fuel and company taxes reach lower deciles too, and if company-type taxes fall partly on wages more households lose (Tables 6.15 and 6.17). The first year is borrowed, not funded: {{PSTAR_ADVANCE_2028_29}} in 2028-29 (provisional), repaid by {{PSTAR_ADVANCE_REPAID_YEAR}}, or {{PHASED_ADVANCE_2028_29}} with a phased start (Tables 6.6 and 6.7). From {{PSTAR_FIRST_SHORTFALL_YEAR}} the five taxes fall short each year, and a joint stress test gives a {{PSTAR_LEVY_PROB_BEFORE_2044_45}} chance of a levy before 2044-45 and 37% to 59% before 2034-35 (Table 6.9, provisional). Stage 1 needs no rise in income-tax rates, but it does need that levy, and the paper does not claim otherwise.

**Objection 4. It costs too much for what it delivers.** A universal payment at the Age Pension rate would need every income-tax rate 30.9 points higher; a lower-rate design modelled by Spies-Butcher, Phillips and Henderson also needed about 12 more points [S57]. A levy on market income alone cannot fund it, and under a doubled taxpayer response or the combined downside no rate rise does. Even Stage 1's monetised net benefit is negative on the complete accounting.

The objection is right on the monetised measures and on a universal payment, which is why the paper legislates no universal rate and does not authorise Stage 3. Stage 1's net benefit on the complete accounting is negative for every design: P\* {{PSTAR_NET_BENEFIT_2028_29}} a year in 2028-29, the targeted package {{PACKAGE_NET_BENEFIT_2028_29}}, and Part 1 −A$24.8bn to −A$4.1bn, or −A$32.5bn to −A$5.7bn with its borrowing charged (Table 6.12). The case for Stage 1 is that it cuts poverty after housing costs from 14.2% to 10.5%; among the designs of equal cost the paper compares, P\* is its choice, weighing poverty against work incentives and fiscal sustainability.

**Objection 5. People will stop working, and income should be earned.** Only 38% of Australians support paying everyone the same amount regardless of work [S43]. OpenResearch found participation fell 3.9 points [S82] and hours by 1.3 a week [S83], and the Coronavirus Supplement cut job-finding by 19% [S113]. The child payment's tapers raise a second earner's participation tax rate to 38% on Part 1's run, against as little as 12% today (Table 5.4).

Stage 1 pays no one regardless of work: the floor keeps JobSeeker's income and activity tests. Most trials find small effects on work. In Germany about 90% of recipients kept working about 40 hours a week [S74], Alaska's dividend had no effect on employment [S94], and Kenya's long-term basic income did not reduce household hours [S81]. The second-earner point stands, and the taper is set by regulation so that couples' labour-supply modelling, due before the Bill, can change it (Recommendation 13). A trial fall in participation above 3 points rules out a universal payment under the Act (Table 9.6). At the earlier 4.2-point estimate that would happen with probability 0.86 to 0.91; at 3.9 points, with probability {{WORK_CAP_ODDS_AT_3_9}}.

**Objection 6. Raising JobSeeker would achieve most of the gain at a fraction of the cost.** ACOSS calls for at least A$600 a week [S197], and in 2023 the Business Council of Australia's chief executive backed a staged rise in JobSeeker [S138]. Raising JobSeeker to about 90% of the pension has been costed at about A$6bn a year [S_ANUJS].

Largely accepted: Stage 1 now does this and more. It raises the working-age payments to the poverty line, at A$19.9bn in 2028-29 with the caseload response; with automatic enrolment that is the floor-first option (A$25.0bn; poverty 12.6%), which the committee can choose (Table 5.11). P\* adds the child payment and the Rent Assistance rise because, at the same budget, they cut poverty further than any universal element.

**Objection 7. The extra spending will raise prices, interest rates and rents**, because it moves income from people who save to people who spend. A Mexican study found cash raised local prices compared with in-kind support [S107], and the RBA estimated that low-income households spent about 0.9 of a one-off payment on non-durables, against 0.3 to 0.7 for all households [S112]. On Part 1's run, if recipients spend 0.3 to 0.6 more of each dollar than those who pay, Stage 1 adds A$13.4bn to A$26.9bn of demand. The Section 6 scenario uses A$20bn to A$40bn, so it errs on the high side.

In that scenario about 17% of Stage 1's household gains are lost to higher repayments and rents, and poverty is 11.6% to 12.0% against 11.3% first-round on Part 1's run (Tables 6.31 and 6.32). It is also why some recipients are worse off after housing costs (Table 6.21). P\* raises Rent Assistance 75%, Stage 1 is legislated well ahead so the RBA can plan, and the rent safeguard raises Rent Assistance further if rents outpace wages (Table 9.6).

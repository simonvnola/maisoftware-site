### What poverty costs Australia

Poverty costs more than the incomes of the people in it. It also costs everyone else, through lower earnings and taxes, more spending on health, child protection, housing and justice, and shorter, less healthy lives. No market prices these costs, and much of the cost falls years later, on people and budgets other than those who could have prevented it. That is a sixth reason for government to act.

No national estimate exists. The only Australian study, for New South Wales, puts the cost of child poverty there at about A$60bn a year, about 7.6% of the state's output. That comprises about A$25bn in direct costs (government services and lower earnings and productivity) and A$34bn for poorer health and shorter lives, valued at 150,000 disability-adjusted life years a year [S_NCOSS]. Studies abroad use different methods. They put the cost of child poverty at nearly 4% to 5.4% of GDP in the United States [S_MCLRANK, S_HOLZER] and at £39.5bn a year in the United Kingdom, an estimate that values no loss of health [S_HIRSCH]. The cost of poverty at all ages to the United Kingdom's public services is about £1 in every £5 they spend [S_BRAMLEY].

#### The poverty trap

Children raised in poverty carry it forward. Australia is fairly mobile by international standards: a child whose parents' income rank is 10 percentiles higher can expect to be about 2 percentiles higher as an adult [S_DEUTSCHER]. But disadvantage concentrates. In Centrelink records for 124,285 young people, 58% of those whose parents had received income support were receiving a payment themselves by age 26, against 32% of those whose parents had not: 1.8 times as likely. The link is strongest where parents received disability, carer or single-parent payments, and the authors conclude that poverty traps, not a "welfare culture", drive it [S_COBBCLARK]. By age seven, children in poverty in New South Wales have lost on average the equivalent of more than a year of schooling [S_NCOSS].

Not all of the gap is caused by low income. Some of it reflects parents' health and education and where families live, which money alone does not change. But the evidence that income itself matters is strong. A systematic review finds that household income has a causal effect on children's cognitive, behavioural and health outcomes, largest in households that start with low incomes [S_COOPER]. In random-assignment experiments in the United States, an extra US$1,000 a year raised young children's achievement by 5% to 6% of a standard deviation [S_DUNCAN], and a study of the earned income tax credit found gains of a similar size [S_DAHL]. One-off payments show little: Australia's Baby Bonus had no average effect on children's test scores, with some evidence of a modest effect for disadvantaged children [S_BABYBONUS]. No study gives a single share of the gap that income can close. The low case in Table 2.3 assumes half of the direct costs could be avoided, and the paper does not assume that any payment removes them all.

#### Poverty weighs on the public health system

People in the lowest socioeconomic group carry 1.6 times the burden of disease of those in the highest [S_AIHW_BOD]. They are admitted to hospital 1.7 times as often for conditions that timely care in the community could have prevented: 3,247 against 1,899 admissions per 100,000 people in 2022-23 [S_AIHW_PPH]. Care also costs more per service in the most disadvantaged areas, in every disease group [S_AIHW_SES]. Australia spent A$290.7bn on health in 2024-25, A$10,614 a person, about 70% of it by governments [S_AIHW_HEA]. Closing the health gap between the most and least disadvantaged Australians was estimated in 2012 to mean 500,000 fewer people with a chronic illness, A$2.3bn a year less spending on hospitals and 5.3 million fewer prescriptions a year, with A$8bn more in earnings and A$4bn less in welfare payments [S_NATSEM].

Much of this cost is paid late, once problems have become crises. Governments spent A$15.2bn in 2018-19 on late intervention for children and young people, including A$5.9bn on child protection, A$1.3bn on mental health and A$1.1bn on physical health [S_COLI]. Spending earlier can cost less. Quality preschool in the year before school returns about A$2 for each A$1 spent in Australia, 41% of it to governments [S_FRONT]. In the United States, intensive early-childhood programmes for disadvantaged children have returned 7% to 10% a year (Perry Preschool) [S_HECKMAN10] and 13.7% a year, with benefits 7.3 times their cost (the Abecedarian and CARE programmes) [S_GARCIA]. Early intervention is not cheaper by default. In an Australian trial of nurse home visiting, right@home, the visits cost A$7,685 more per family and were not yet cost-effective when the children were three, although their effects on parenting lasted to age five [S_RIGHTHOME]. The returns come where programmes are well targeted and evaluated, and they take years to arrive. Table 2.4 sets out the evidence.

#### What this means for the proposal

The cost of poverty is large next to the cost of reducing it. Ending child poverty after housing costs with an exact top-up to the line would cost {{ZP_K_CUR_TOTAL}} a year from current rules, or {{ZP_K_STAR_TOTAL}} on top of P\* (Table 6.ZP), against a cost of child poverty of {{COP_CHILD_TOTAL}} a year (Table 2.3). P\*'s {{COP_LIFTED_CHILDREN}} fewer children in poverty are associated, in the long run, with {{COP_ASSOC_PSTAR}} a year of that cost.

These figures cannot be set against each other as a Budget saving. Most of the cost of poverty is lost earnings, health and years of life that fall on people over decades, and only part of it returns to the Budget as tax and lower spending. Lifting a child just above the line does not remove their disadvantage. So the paper does not claim that its payments pay for themselves. It claims that poverty's cost to everyone else is a reason to act that Budget figures leave out, and that the evaluation of Stage 1 should measure it (Recommendation 18).

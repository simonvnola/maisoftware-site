### Who gains: three designs at the same budget

At the same budget, P\* leaves 10.5% of people in poverty after housing costs on the relative line, against 11.3% for Part 1 and 9.0% for the targeted package charged the same income effect (Table 5.7). It has less poverty than Part 1 in every group shown: children 6.6% against 7.2%, sole-parent households 14.8% against 16.4%, people aged 67 and over 11.6% against 12.1%, and households relying on the Disability Support Pension 52.6% against 68.2%. That holds on all three random seeds and all three calibrations of the household model. Part 1 leaves 0.20 million more people in poverty.

The targeted package does better still on poverty, with 0.40 million fewer people in poverty than P\* and less poverty in every group in every robustness run. It does so by adding an earned-income credit and a larger FTB Part A, which stack withdrawal rates on low- and middle-income working families, and by automatic enrolment.

Preferring P\* to the package is a value judgement, and the paper states its price on every method it uses. The package leaves about 0.40 million fewer people in poverty (1.5 points). On the paper's method it is A$2.0bn to A$4.3bn a year ahead of P\*; on the complete accounting it is between A$1.9bn a year behind and A$2.3bn ahead; with the Green Book's weights it is about A$10.7bn a year ahead. It breaks even at lower distributional weights: 1.3 to 2.4 on the paper's method and 2.0 to 3.8 on the complete accounting, against P\*'s 1.5 to 3.3 and 2.3 to 4.9 (Table 6.12, provisional). On average the package also treats working families better: second earners' mean participation tax rate is 28.9% against 33.0% under P\*, 22.4% of second earners face 40% or more against 31.6%, and 21.8% of higher earners in families with children face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more against 41.6% (Table 5.7). P\*'s work-incentive advantage is only at the extreme: 1.3% of higher earners face 60% or more under P\* against 14.2% under the package, and no second earner faces 60% or more against 6.8%. The paper's second reason is the fiscal path. The package's costs grow faster, so it needs A$108.1bn of levies to 2045-46 against A$36.5bn for P\*, and a levy before 2044-45 is more likely, 87% against 74% in the joint stress test (Tables 6.6 and 6.9; model results Table B5; provisional). The paper judges that avoiding those very high rates and the larger levy is worth the price. Parliament may reasonably judge otherwise, and should choose the package if it weights poverty above them.

A variant of P\* with automatic enrolment, re-costed to Part 1's budget, must cut the child supplement to A$5,734. It has lower overall poverty than P\* (10.3%) but higher child poverty (7.0% against 6.6%), and its levies to 2045-46 are larger (A$65.4bn against A$36.5bn). It is not recommended.

### Effective tax rates and second earners

P\* keeps Part 1's 18-cent taper, so its effective tax rates are close to Part 1's. Of higher earners in families with children, 41.6% face an effective marginal tax rate of 50% or more, most of them at exactly 50% (the taper on the 30% rate, with the Medicare levy); 14.7% face more than 50% and 1.3% face 60% or more (Table 5.7). The targeted package stacks a 20-cent taper on its FTB increment and the credit's phase-out on FTB, so fewer families sit at exactly 50% but more face very high rates: 21.8% above 50% and 14.2% at 60% or more.

Second earners keep less under P\*'s taper than today. Their mean participation tax rate, the share of their earnings lost to tax and withdrawn payments, is 33.0%, against 28.9% under the package, 32.9% under Part 1 and 27.3% under current rules. None faces 60% or more under P\*, against 6.8% under the package. Against Part 1, P\*'s rates are much the same: slightly fewer higher earners at high rates, but slightly more second earners at 40% or more (31.6% against 29.4%). So the value judgement that favours P\* concerns the package, not Part 1, which P\* beats on poverty. On Part 1's run, whose taper P\* keeps, a second earner taking a A$50,000 job keeps 62 cents of each dollar whether their partner earns A$100,000, A$130,000 or A$160,000; today they keep 61, 72 and 88 cents (Table 5.4). The taper is set by regulation, so couples' labour-supply modelling, due by 30 June 2027, can change it; that modelling will cost second earners who stop work (Recommendation 13).

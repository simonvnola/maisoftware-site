**Derived figures:**
- The domain costs excluding the A$34bn sum to 19 + 5.3 + 3.0 + 1.1 + 0.855 = **A$29.3bn**. That is more than the A$25–26bn direct total, which fits the report's overlap adjustment. **[calc]**
- The A$34bn divided by 150,000 DALYs is about **A$226,700 per DALY**. This is almost exactly the Office of Impact Analysis value of a statistical life year in 2022 dollars (A$227,000; see 4.8), so the report probably used the OIA value. This is my inference and is not confirmed. **[calc]**
- **Per child in poverty, per year:**
  - Total: A$60bn ÷ 278,000 = **A$215,800**
  - Direct only (A$25bn): **A$89,900**
  - Direct only (A$26bn): **A$93,500**
  - All three are **[calc]**.
- **How to read the per-child ratio.** The A$60bn is an annual cost for the whole population. It must include adults whose current outcomes stem from childhood poverty: 150,000 DALYs spread over 278,000 children would be 0.54 DALY per child per year, which is implausible. So the ratio is a **steady-state ratio**. If child poverty stays roughly constant, it approximates the lifetime, undiscounted cost attributable to one child-year of poverty. It is not cash spent on each poor child each year. The paper should word it that way.
- **Price year.** Probably 2024 dollars, but the snippets did not confirm it.

## 3. The poverty trap: intergenerational persistence in Australia

### 3.1 Deutscher and Mazumder (2020), "Intergenerational mobility across Australia and the stability of regional estimates", *Labour Economics* 66: 101861, doi:10.1016/j.labeco.2020.101861

Sources: https://ideas.repec.org/a/eee/labeco/v66y2020ics0927537120300658.html ; working paper at https://economics.uq.edu.au/node/6582

- Rank-rank slope: **about 0.21–0.22** in the published version. The working paper gives **0.215**. SNIPPET
- Intergenerational income elasticity: **0.185** for total income (working paper). The published value was not seen. SNIPPET
- Data: tax records for more than 1 million people born 1978–82. SNIPPET
- Comparison: roughly on par with Sweden, against about 0.4 or more in the US. SNIPPET
- Interpretation: a child whose parents are 10 percentile points higher can expect to be about 2 points higher. Australia is relatively mobile, so the "trap" is concentrated rather than general. Persistence is strongest in specific forms of disadvantage (see 3.2).

### 3.2 Cobb-Clark, Dahmann, Salamanca and Zhu, "Intergenerational disadvantage: Learning about equal opportunity from social assistance receipt", *Labour Economics* 79 (2022): 102276; IZA DP 11070 (2017)

Sources: https://ses.library.usyd.edu.au/handle/2123/29614 ; https://www.unimelb.edu.au/newsroom/news/2017/october/children-more-likely-to-receive-welfare-benefits-if-their-parents-do

- Young people are **1.8 times** more likely to need social assistance if their parents received it. This is the IZA 2017 figure; the published version may differ. SNIPPET
- By age 26: **58%** of children of welfare-receiving parents are themselves receiving a payment, against **32%** of children of non-recipients. SNIPPET
- Data: 18 years of Centrelink records covering **124,285** people. SNIPPET
- Children of recipients also receive more intensive support: about **A$12,000 more over 8 years**. SNIPPET
- Persistence is strongest when parents received disability, carer or single-parent payments, and weaker when parents received unemployment benefits. The authors conclude that disadvantage is "more likely driven by poverty traps than welfare cultures". SNIPPET

### 3.3 Causal evidence that income itself improves children's outcomes

- **Cooper and Stewart (2021, online 2020), *Child Indicators Research*, "Does household income affect children's outcomes? A systematic review of the evidence".** https://eprints.lse.ac.uk/107029/
  - Finds strong support for a positive **causal** effect of income on cognitive, social-behavioural and health outcomes, "particularly in households with low income to begin with". SNIPPET
  - Effects are larger in experimental and quasi-experimental studies than in fixed-effects studies. SNIPPET
  - Income also improves maternal mental health, parenting and the home environment. SNIPPET
  - The 2017 CASE update reports cognitive gains of **5–27% of a standard deviation** for the income changes studied (CASEpaper 203: https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/103494/1/casepaper203.pdf). SNIPPET
  - The authors judge that £1,000 on household income has "a similar effect on schooling outcomes as £1,000 on schools". SNIPPET
- **Dahl and Lochner (2012), *AER* 102(5): 1927–56.** https://www.nber.org/papers/W11279
  - Uses EITC expansions as an instrument for income.
  - The 2005 NBER version found that **US$1,000 raises maths by 2.1% and reading by 3.6% of a standard deviation**.
  - Dahl's 2015 restatement: **US$1,000 (2015 US$) raises combined maths and reading by 4.4% of a standard deviation** (https://econweb.ucsd.edu/~gdahl/papers/insight-EITC.pdf).
  - The exact AER table value was not seen. SNIPPET
- **Duncan, Morris and Rodrigues (2011), *Developmental Psychology*, doi:10.1037/a0023875.** Draws on 16 welfare-to-work random-assignment experiments. **US$1,000 a year raises young children's achievement by 5–6% of a standard deviation.** SNIPPET. https://cepa.stanford.edu/cepa.stanford.edu/sites/default/files/workshops/DuncanMorrisRodrigues2011_doesmoneymatter.pdf
- **Australian causal studies:**
  - Deutscher and Breunig (ANU, Baby Bonus natural experiment, difference-in-differences): no aggregate improvement in Grade 3 test scores, but "some evidence of a modest effect for children from disadvantaged backgrounds". SNIPPET. https://www.lifecoursecentre.org.au/wp-content/uploads/2018/06/2016-25-LCC-Working-Paper-Deutscher-and-Breunig-amended-2.pdf
  - IZA DP 10525: the A$3,000 Baby Bonus "was not effective" for the average child's learning, socio-emotional or physical outcomes. SNIPPET. https://docs.iza.org/dp10525.pdf
  - Takeaway: one-off lump sums show small or zero average effects. Sustained income to low-income families is where the causal evidence is strongest.
- **Defensible causal share.** No source gives a single "share of the gap closed by money". The options with support are:
  - The NCOSS attributable-fraction approach, for example 20% of youth offending attributed to child poverty.
  - The quasi-experimental effect sizes of about 5–6% of a standard deviation per US$1,000 a year. These imply that a sustained US$3,000–4,000 a year closes roughly 0.15–0.25 SD. Against a poor/non-poor attainment gap of about 0.5–0.8 SD, that is roughly **a quarter to a half**. **[calc; the size of the gap is my assumption and is not sourced.]**
  - On that basis the estimate below uses **50% (low case)** and **100% of NCOSS direct costs (central)**. NCOSS appears already to apply attributable fractions; this was not confirmed.

- Gap for families with children: **A$464 a week**. This appears in one secondary snippet only and was not confirmed. SNIPPET

## Suggested per-person estimate

All figures are annual, in approximate 2024 A$, unadjusted for price years. They are **steady-state** costs: they assume poverty rates stay roughly where they are. The per-child figure means the annual societal cost of child poverty divided by the number of children now in poverty. That is close to the lifetime cost attributable to one child-year of poverty.

### A. Per child in poverty

Each case starts from NCOSS: 278,000 NSW children in poverty (§1.1).

| Case | Basis | Arithmetic | A$ per child per year |
|---|---|---|---|
| Low | NCOSS direct costs only, with a 50% causal-share discount for residual confounding (§3.3; the discount is my assumption) | 25bn × 0.5 ÷ 278,000 | **≈ 45,000** |
| Central | NCOSS direct costs (government services plus lost productivity), no DALY valuation | 25bn ÷ 278,000 | **≈ 90,000** |
| High | NCOSS total, including 150,000 DALYs valued at about A$227k each | 60bn ÷ 278,000 | **≈ 216,000** |

Cross-checks:
- McLaughlin and Rank: about **US$71,000** per poor child (2015 US$; the child count is mine). This includes crime victimisation and health, and sits between the low and central cases.
- Hirsch (UK): about **£9,400**. This is mostly fiscal and is well below the low case.
- The NCOSS figures are high by international standards. NCOSS puts the cost at 7.6–7.8% of GSP, against 4–5.4% of GDP in US studies. A cautious paper could make the low case its headline figure.

### B. Per person in poverty, all ages

The total combines two parts:
- **(i) Intergenerational cost:** the per-child figure × 757,000 children (ACOSS, ages 0–14, which is conservative).
- **(ii) Excess health-system cost for adults in poverty:** a premium on average health spending of A$10,614 per person (AIHW 2024–25) for 2,949,000 adults. That adult count is 3,706,000 − 757,000. **[calc]**

The size of the premium is an **assumption** bounded by the evidence:
- 20% (low)
- 30% (central)
- 60% (high), matching the 1.6 burden ratio and the 1.7 preventable-hospitalisation ratio of the lowest to the highest quintile (§4.2)

| Case | (i) Children | (ii) Adults' health premium | National total | ÷ 3,706,000 people |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Low | 45,000 × 757k = A$34.0bn | 2,123 × 2.949m = A$6.3bn | A$40.3bn | **≈ A$10,900 per person** |
| Central | 89,928 × 757k = A$68.1bn | 3,184 × 2.949m = A$9.4bn | A$77.5bn | **≈ A$20,900 per person** |
| High | 215,827 × 757k = A$163.4bn | 6,368 × 2.949m = A$18.8bn | A$182.2bn | **≈ A$49,200 per person** |

Caveats:
- Part (ii) partly overlaps with the NCOSS health domain, because NCOSS counts adults affected by childhood poverty. For a conservative headline, drop (ii) or use the low case.
- The comparable UK public-cost-only figure is about **£5,800 per person** (Bramley et al. 2016; §2.5).
- Scale check: the central national total of about A$77.5bn would be roughly **2.7% of GDP**, against 4–5.4% in the US studies. This uses an Australian GDP of about A$2.8–2.9tn, which is my approximation and not sourced; the lead should verify it.

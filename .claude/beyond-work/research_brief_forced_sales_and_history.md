# Research brief: forced sales in Australia today, and the industrial revolution's labour lessons

## Context

## A. Forced sales in Australia today

The paper proposes monitoring "forced sales as a share of annual dwelling sales" against the ABS count of dwelling transfers (560,005 in 2025-26), and needs today's baseline.

1. The number of mortgagee-in-possession sales (lender repossession sales) of residential dwellings in Australia in the latest year and each of the past five years, nationally and by state. Possible sources: CoreLogic/Cotality or SQM Research mortgagee-in-possession listing counts; state Supreme Court possession orders (NSW, Victoria, Queensland); APRA or RBA publications; lender annual reports.
2. Any measure of distressed or hardship-driven sales that are not repossessions (owners selling under financial stress before default), from the RBA, CoreLogic/Cotality, Digital Finance Analytics or academic work, with its definition.
3. Each figure as a share of annual dwelling transfers, and the peak share since 2000 (for example in the early 1990s, 2008-09 or 2020), so the paper can set a threshold as a multiple of the normal level.

## B. The industrial revolution: living standards and working conditions

The paper argues that technological change raised output long before it raised most people's living standards, and that for decades many people were pushed into poverty or dangerous and demeaning work. Verify and give the key findings, with figures where the source states them, from:

1. Robert C. Allen, "Engels' pause: Technical change, capital accumulation, and inequality in the British industrial revolution", Explorations in Economic History 46(4), 2009: how long real wages stagnated while output per worker rose, and the period.
2. Charles H. Feinstein, "Pessimism perpetuated: real wages and the standard of living in Britain during and after the industrial revolution", Journal of Economic History 58(3), 1998: the growth of real earnings over 1770–1830 and after.
3. Jane Humphries, Childhood and Child Labour in the British Industrial Revolution, Cambridge University Press, 2010: the extent and ages of child labour.
4. The UK Factory Act 1833 and later factory and mines acts: what they limited, and when.
5. Any standard source on the health and mortality costs of early industrial towns (for example Simon Szreter's work on urban mortality), and on the timing of social insurance (Bismarck's 1880s schemes; Britain's 1911 National Insurance Act; Australia's 1908 age pension).
6. Any Australian parallel worth citing (for example nineteenth-century colonial labour conditions or the Harvester judgment of 1907 setting a living wage).

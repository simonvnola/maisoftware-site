# Research brief: asset-price and household-debt effects of an AI displacement shock in Australia

## Context

You are researching for a policy paper, "Beyond Work: Preparing a Basic Income for Australia's AI Transition" (Mai Software, working draft, 1 November 2026). The paper models an Australian income-support floor (Stage 1) and the fiscal effects of an AI displacement shock over 2028-29 to 2045-46. Its model has household tenure and mortgage debt by income quintile, but no household asset data, no investor households and no asset-price feedback. A new section (chapter 6, "Second-round effects of a displacement: claims, debt and asset prices") therefore rests on placeholders and unverified sources, listed below. Your job is to replace each placeholder with a sourced figure and to verify or replace each named source, so that every number the section quotes has a citation a parliamentary committee could check.

The scenarios you are sizing: by 2035-36, a moderate shock displaces 0.67 million workers (0.95 million extra recipients) and a severe shock 1.77 million (2.53 million). Of displaced households, about 0.35 million (moderate) hold a mortgage. On the floor, 64,000 (moderate) to 170,000 (severe) of them would pay more than half their income in repayments; under current JobSeeker rules 85,000 to 224,000. The paper's illustrative forced-sale calculation assumes those households all sell within a year, against a placeholder of 550,000 dwelling sales a year, with a price elasticity to forced sales assumed at 0.25 to 1.0.

## Rules

- Australian data wherever it exists; international evidence only where Australian evidence does not.
- For every figure: the number, its unit, the year or period, the exact source (organisation, title, date, URL, and the table or page), and one sentence on how it was measured. Prefer primary sources (ABS, RBA, APRA, DSS, Services Australia, PBO, Treasury, AIHW, CoreLogic/Cotality, HILDA) over news reports.
- Where sources disagree, give the range and say why they differ. Where no source exists, say so plainly rather than estimate.
- Do not infer a figure from a related one without saying you have done so and showing the arithmetic.
- Quote the sentence or table cell you rely on where you can.

## What to find

### A. Dwelling sales volumes (replaces the 550,000 placeholder)
1. Annual residential dwelling sales (transfers) in Australia, latest full year and the five-year average, from ABS (Total Value of Dwellings, or property transfer counts) or CoreLogic/Cotality; state which measure (settled sales, transfers, listings) and whether off-the-plan and non-market transfers are included.
2. Annual new listings, if a separate series exists, since forced sales affect listings before sales.

### B. Price response to forced or distressed sales (replaces the 0.25 to 1.0 assumption)
Verify, summarise and give the exact estimate and its units from each of these; add any Australian study on the same question:
1. Campbell, Giglio and Pathak (2011), "Forced sales and house prices", American Economic Review: the discount on forced sales and the spillover to nearby prices.
2. Mian, Sufi and Trebbi (2015), "Foreclosures, house prices, and the real economy", Journal of Finance: the elasticity of house prices to foreclosure volumes.
3. Anenberg and Kung (2014), "Estimates of the size and source of price declines due to nearby foreclosures", American Economic Review.
4. Saunders and Tulip (2019), "A model of the Australian housing market", RBA Research Discussion Paper 2019-01: what it implies for prices under a demand or supply shock of the size above.
5. RBA Financial Stability Review boxes on distressed sales, mortgage arrears and forced sales (2020 to 2026): any Australian estimate of how arrears translate into forced sales and how forced sales move prices.
Then state, with reasoning, a defensible range for the fall in Australian dwelling prices if 64,000 and if 170,000 extra forced sales occurred within a year, as a percentage of the annual sales volume from A, and whether the effect is national or concentrated in the suburbs where displaced workers live.

### C. Household debt and buffers (replaces "not in the paper's sources")
1. The share of owner-occupier mortgage holders with less than one, three and six months of repayments in offset and redraw buffers, from the RBA Financial Stability Review (latest, and the series back to 2022 if available).
2. The share of mortgaged households with investment property loans, and the number of households with investment property, from ABS Survey of Income and Housing or HILDA; the median investor loan balance and gearing.
3. Current mortgage arrears (90+ days) rates and the historical peak, from APRA or the RBA.
4. Any evidence on how long unemployed mortgage holders carry a loan before selling (hardship arrangement durations; APRA or lender data; the RBA's work on the 2020 deferrals).

### D. Means-test barriers for displaced workers (replaces "not estimable")
1. The current JobSeeker assets test thresholds for homeowners and non-homeowners, singles and couples, and the liquid assets waiting period thresholds and maximum weeks, from the DSS rates list or the Social Security Guide, with the date.
2. The distribution of non-home, non-superannuation assets (shares, investment property, cash) among employed adults aged 25 to 64, from ABS Survey of Income and Housing or HILDA's wealth module: the share who would exceed the assets test threshold, and the share whose liquid assets exceed the waiting-period threshold. If no published cut gives this directly, say what table comes closest and what would have to be assumed.
3. The partner income test cut-out for JobSeeker, with its date.

### E. Claims processing capacity (replaces "compared with job losses only")
1. Annual JobSeeker and Youth Allowance claims lodged and granted, and the current median processing time against the service standard, from Services Australia annual reports or Senate estimates (latest three years).
2. The peak monthly claims and processing times in March to June 2020, and what Services Australia did to meet them (staffing, automated grants), as the one Australian precedent for a surge.

### F. Budget channels
1. The bank share of the excess profits tax base in PBO election commitment costing ECR-2025-3194 (40% excess profits tax on companies with turnover above A$100 million): does the costing report the contribution by sector, and if so the banks' share.
2. State stamp duty and land tax revenue (latest year, by state) and any published sensitivity of stamp duty revenue to dwelling prices and turnover (state budget papers or Parliamentary Budget Offices).

### G. Rents after a price fall
Australian evidence on what happens to rents when prices fall and investors sell to owner-occupiers (RBA, AHURI, Productivity Commission, CoreLogic): does the rental stock shrink, and do rents rise or fall in the following two years? Give the periods studied.

## Deliverable

A report in this order: a one-page summary table with every figure the paper should quote (item, value, unit, period, source), then one section per heading A to G with the findings and quotations, then a list of every item you could not source and why. Mark each figure as Australian or international, and each source as primary or secondary. Do not pad; if an item takes one line, give it one line.

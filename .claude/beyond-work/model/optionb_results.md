## 0. First, a correction: P* as printed is not at Part 1 alone's budget

household_r13.py solved P*'s per-child amount so that its household-cash family cost, plus the floor, automatic enrolment and Rent Assistance, equalled Part 1's *fiscal* net cost (A$62.4bn). The fiscal model costs Part 1's child payment from the PBO's FTB path (A$18.6bn of FTB replaced) plus the household model's rule flows, including the prior-year timing cost (A$2.1bn plus the A$0.8bn income-fall review); the household model's own current FTB is A$23.2bn. So the printed P* was given the A$3.1bn by which the household model under-costs the child payment against the fiscal model, and was not charged its own prior-year timing. On the fiscal model's basis it costs **A$6.2bn more than Part 1 in 2028-29** (A$1.1bn excluding the A$5.1bn of enrolment, which Part 1 alone's fiscal path does not carry). Re-costed at Part 1's cost on the fiscal basis, the per-child amount is **A$6,750** without enrolment (the plan's P*: floor, child payment without the supplement-only schedule, Rent Assistance +75%) and **A$5,734** with enrolment, against A$6,973 as printed. Everything below uses the re-costed P* (without enrolment) as the recommended Stage 1, and shows the printed P* only for comparison.

## 2. Robustness: three seeds and three calibrations

## 9. Findings the author can quote, and the figures for the summary (option B)

**P* re-costed.** P* as first printed was not at Part 1 alone's budget: on the fiscal model's own basis it cost A$6.2bn more in 2028-29, because its child payment was costed against the household model's higher current FTB and was not charged its prior-year timing cost. Re-costed at Part 1's cost, P* pays A$6,750 a child a year (Part 1's supplement is A$7,452) and keeps the floor and Rent Assistance +75%.

**Poverty.** At the same cost as Part 1, P* cuts poverty after housing costs to 10.5% on the relative line, against 11.3% for Part 1 and 9.0% for the targeted package: 0.20 million fewer people in poverty than Part 1, and 0.40 million more than the package. Child poverty is 6.6% (Part 1 7.2%), sole-parent households 14.8% (16.4%) and people aged 67 and over 11.6% (12.1%).

**Robustness.** Across 9 household-model configurations (three random seeds and three calibrations), P* has less poverty than Part 1 in every group shown and a higher first-year net benefit in every one (A$0.8bn to A$3.1bn a year on the complete accounting); the targeted package has less poverty than P* in every group in every configuration.

**Cost and borrowing.** P* costs A$62.0bn in 2028-29 (1.86% of GDP), rising to A$130.8bn in 2045-46. The five taxes fall A$20.7bn short in the first year; that is borrowing, repaid with interest by 30 June 2032. Surpluses then build to A$45bn and are drawn down from the late 2030s.

**The levy.** On central yields a levy is first needed in 2043-44 (in 2044-45 if Rent Assistance grows on the fiscal model's path rather than with the model's rents), totalling A$37bn to 2045-46 and peaking at 0.62% of market income; Part 1's is 2044-45 and A$26bn. The five taxes must yield 100.2% of their costings for no levy before 2044-45.

**Stress test.** Drawing tax yield, behavioural responses, cost overruns, the household calibration and the AI path together, the chance of a levy before 2044-45 is 74% for P* (73% for Part 1) and before 2034-35 59% (58%); with symmetric uncertainty alone it is 48% and 37%. The most likely first levy year is 2032-33, when an advance not repaid by then would be levied at once. P* carries the same funding risk as Part 1; the package carries more.

**A phased start.** Starting the floor and Rent Assistance in 2028-29 and the child payment a year later removes the first-year borrowing: 2028-29 costs A$24.8bn against A$41.3bn of taxes, and on central yields no levy is needed to 2045-46. The price is a year without the child payment: A$26.4bn of family payments above the FTB families keep (a budget saving of A$37.3bn).

**After housing costs.** The no-worse-off rule protects payments under today's rules, not income after housing costs. In the paper's rent and interest-rate scenario, 1.11 million households on current payments (2.30 million people) are worse off under Part 1, by about A$933 a year; under P* 0.70 million (1.65 million people, about A$770): mostly Disability Support Pension households (0.28 million) and Age Pension households that own or are buying their home (0.26 million), whom Rent Assistance does not reach.

**Poverty after the housing scenario.** After the scenario's rent and interest-rate effects (central case), poverty on the relative line is 11.1% with P*, 11.7% with Part 1 and 9.8% with the package.

**Who is better off after the taxes.** Before the funding taxes 62% of households gain from P*; after them 35% are better off (33% by A$5 a week or more), against 33% under Part 1 and 42% under the package.

**Net benefit.** On the paper's complete accounting every design's first-year net benefit is negative at its 1.5 to 2.0 weights; it breaks even at a weight of 2.3 to 4.9 on gains to households in poverty for P* (2.8 to 6.1 for Part 1, 2.0 to 3.8 for the package). With the Green Book's standard weights every design is positive: P* A$40bn to A$49bn, Part 1 A$37bn to A$46bn, the package A$51bn to A$60bn, so standard weights widen the package's lead.

**The trial's work-effort cap.** At OpenResearch's revised 3.9-point fall in participation, the Australian trial would trip the 3-point cap with probability 0.79 to 0.84 (0.86 to 0.91 at 4.2). The costed labour response, 16 cents a dollar, is an income response and stays.
